What it supplies

The Latin witness, in this repository, for what Newton wrote in Book I about division, in the first edition. It answers a gap found by the adversarial review of 2026-09-23: the paper quoted the Section I scholium from a sibling repository’s passage store, with no witness here.

  • Lemma XI, Corollary 3 (1687 numbering). The ultimate curvilinear areas between tangent and arc are “duæ tertiæ partes triangulorum rectilineorum”, two thirds of the rectilinear triangles, and the segments “partes tertiæ”, one third, “ex natura Parabolæ”. These are the thirds the Planck-gap notes use: the inertial–parabola area is two thirds of the tangent triangle, the chord’s segment one third.
  • The scholium after Lemma XI, on contact angles. Series of contact angles can be continued without end, and “inter duos quosvis ex his angulis potest series utrinq; in infinitum pergens angulorum intermediorum inseri”; between any two of the new series another can be inserted, “Neq; novit natura limitem”: nature knows no limit. Newton’s own statement, in Book I, that insertion has no end in the geometry.
  • The scholium closing Section I, first paragraph. The lemmas were put first “ut effugerem tædium deducendi perplexas demonstrationes, more veterum Geometrarum, ad absurdum”: to avoid the tedium of the ancient geometers’ reductio, the method of exhaustion that Archimedes credits to Eudoxus; the method of indivisibles is shorter, “Sed quoniam durior est indivisibilium Hypothesis”, Newton prefers limits, and where he later speaks of particles he means “evanescentia divisibilia”.
  • The same scholium, third paragraph. The objection that given ultimate ratios give ultimate magnitudes, so that “quantitas omnis constabit ex indivisibilibus, contra quam Euclides de incommensurabilibus, in libro decimo Elementorum, demonstravit”, is answered by limits, and the scholium closes “cave intelligas quantitates magnitudine determinatas, sed cogita semper diminuendas sine limite”.
