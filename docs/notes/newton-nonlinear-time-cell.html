A nonlinear Newton time cell: controlled fluctuations and induced stiffness

Markdown source · PDF

Result, 2026-10-27; written derivation, unrefereed. An arbitrary intermediate time can be eliminated exactly from a positive Euclidean mechanical weight. For a convex potential, the normalized nonlinear conditional law has an explicit fluctuation bound, and the retained interaction has bounded curvature even when the original force derivative is unbounded. Both integration and classical minimization soften the curvature. They differ at a quartic flat centre: positive supplied \(\hbar\) generates strictly positive quadratic curvature, while classical elimination retains zero quadratic curvature.

This is a concrete \(0+1\) use of Balaban’s instruction to retain the full normalized interaction and its large-field contribution. It supplies a nonlinear calculation beyond the Gaussian reference of the time-blocking note. The positive weight and its action multiplier are premises. An experimentally inferred positive curvature would need additional arguments before being identified with a necessary universal quantum action.

Source motivation: Dimock I, sections 2.1, 4.5 and Theorem 14, and Dimock II, Theorem 3.1 and sections 3.13–3.14 (selected passages read). The old quartic limit theorem, section 3, uses \(V=\lambda q^4\); here the convention is \(V=\lambda q^4/4\). The two normalizations must not be mixed.

1. The complete one-vertex weight

Let \(m,u,v,\hbar>0\), \(T=u+v\), and fix positions \(x,y\). Put

\[ \mu=\frac{vx+uy}{T},\qquad a=\frac{mT}{uv},\qquad w=\frac T2. \tag{1} \]

Here \(a\) has units of action per length squared and \(w\) is a time. Completing the kinetic square gives

\[ \frac{m(z-x)^2}{2u}+\frac{m(y-z)^2}{2v} =\frac{m(y-x)^2}{2T}+\frac a2(z-\mu)^2. \tag{2} \]

The symmetric Euclidean action also includes \(uV(x)/2+wV(z)+vV(y)/2\). Define

\[ I_\hbar(\mu)=\sqrt{\frac{a}{2\pi\hbar}} \int_{\mathbb R}\exp\left[-\frac{F_\mu(z)}{\hbar}\right]dz, \quad F_\mu(z)=\frac a2(z-\mu)^2+wV(z),\quad A_\hbar=-\hbar\log I_\hbar. \tag{3} \]

Then the integrated two-cell kernel, with the free normalization at each cell, is exactly

\[ \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar T}} \exp\left[-\frac1\hbar\left\{ \frac{m(y-x)^2}{2T}+\frac u2V(x)+\frac v2V(y) +A_\hbar(\mu)\right\}\right]. \tag{4} \]

We retain the whole function \(A_\hbar\); it is not generally a quartic polynomial. The normalized conditional law \(\nu_{\hbar,\mu}\) of \(Z\) is \(e^{-F_\mu/\hbar}dz\) divided by its integral. No unnormalized action cost is used as a probability estimate.

For comparison define

\[A_0(\mu)=\min_z F_\mu(z).\tag{5}\]

This is a classical Euclidean stationary elimination. It is not the real-time Newton action: for real time the potential sign in (2)–(4) changes and an oscillatory integral replaces the positive weight. In particular, these probability bounds cannot simply be continued to real time. No \(c\) or relativistic premise is used here.

2. Convexity gives normalized control

Assume \(V\in C^2(\mathbb R)\) is convex, with sufficient growth regularity for the following integrations by parts. Polynomials with nonnegative second derivative satisfy the hypotheses. The Gaussian kinetic term makes \(F_\mu\) strongly convex, so it has one minimizer \(z_*(\mu)\) and \(F_\mu''\ge a\). All moments needed below are finite.

Proposition 1. For \(n=1,2,\ldots\) and \(r>0\),

\[ \mathbb E_{\nu_{\hbar,\mu}}(Z-z_*)^{2n} \le(2n-1)!!\left(\frac\hbar a\right)^n, \qquad \nu_{\hbar,\mu}(|Z-z_*|\ge r) \le2\exp\left[-\frac{ar^2}{2\hbar}\right]. \tag{6} \]

Also

\[ \operatorname{Var}(Z)\le\frac\hbar a,\qquad A_\hbar'=a(\mu-\mathbb EZ),\qquad A_\hbar''=a-\frac{a^2}{\hbar}\operatorname{Var}(Z),\qquad 0\le A_\hbar''\le a. \tag{7} \]

Proof. Write \(Y=Z-z_*\). Strong convexity and \(F_\mu'(z_*)=0\) imply \(YF_\mu'(z_*+Y)\ge aY^2\). Integration by parts gives

\[ \mathbb E[Y^{2n-1}F_\mu'(Z)] =(2n-1)\hbar\mathbb EY^{2n-2}. \tag{8} \]

The left side is at least \(a\mathbb EY^{2n}\). Induction proves the moment bound. Expanding \(\cosh(tY)\) in its nonnegative even powers gives \(\mathbb E\cosh(tY)\le e^{\hbar t^2/(2a)}\). Since \(e^{t|Y|}\le e^{tY}+e^{-tY}\), exponential Markov inequality with \(t=ar/\hbar\) gives the tail bound.

For variance, take independent copies \(Z,Z'\). Integration by parts also gives \(\mathbb EF_\mu'=0\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z,F_\mu'(Z))=\hbar\). Consequently

\[ \hbar=\frac12\mathbb E[(Z-Z')(F_\mu'(Z)-F_\mu'(Z'))] \ge a\operatorname{Var}(Z). \tag{9} \]

Differentiating the normalized integral (3) gives the two derivative identities in (7); in particular \(\partial_\mu\mathbb EZ=a\operatorname{Var}(Z)/\hbar\). This proves the curvature bounds. \(\square\)

If additionally \(0\le V''\le K\), the same two-copy identity with the upper slope bound gives

\[ \frac\hbar{a+wK}\le\operatorname{Var}(Z)\le\frac\hbar a, \qquad 0\le A_\hbar''\le\frac{awK}{a+wK}. \tag{10} \]

These are bounds for the full normalized nonlinear conditional law. They require neither a quadratic approximation nor a small quartic coupling.

The function \(A_\hbar/w\), viewed as a potential of the coordinate \(\mu\), has force derivative bounded by \(a/(mw)=2/(uv)\). This is a mathematically well-defined coarse potential. Identifying its gradient with the force of a physical Newtonian body is a further modeling choice: (4) is an endpoint kernel, not an autonomous Newton equation derived from it.

3. Classical elimination has the same curvature bound

The minimizer satisfies

\[a(z_*-\mu)+wV'(z_*)=0,\qquad z_*'=\frac a{a+wV''(z_*)}. \tag{11} \]

The implicit function theorem and direct differentiation yield

\[ A_0'=a(\mu-z_*),\qquad A_0''=\frac{awV''(z_*)}{a+wV''(z_*)},\qquad 0\le A_0''\le a. \tag{12} \]

Thus bounded coarse curvature is already possible in classical elimination. For the convex quartic \(V=\lambda z^4/4\), \(\lambda>0\), the positive-\(\hbar\) interaction approaches this classical envelope with an explicit bound:

\[ 0\le A_\hbar-A_0 \le\frac{3w\lambda z_*^2\hbar}{2a} +\frac{3w\lambda\hbar^2}{4a^2}. \tag{13} \]

Proof. After centering the integral at \(z_*\), let \(Y\sim N(0,\hbar/a)\) and \(R_V=V(z_*+Y)-V(z_*)-V'(z_*)Y\ge0\). Cancellation of the linear terms in \(F_\mu\) gives

\[ A_\hbar=A_0-\hbar\log\mathbb E e^{-wR_V/\hbar}. \tag{14} \]

The expectation is at most one, and Jensen’s inequality bounds the additional action by \(w\mathbb ER_V\). For the quartic, the Gaussian second and fourth moments give exactly the right side of (13). This proves local uniform convergence as \(\hbar\downarrow0\). \(\square\)

Classical minimization and positive integration therefore share one regularity mechanism. A criterion requiring only a finite ODE Lipschitz constant would not distinguish them in this convex cell.

4. The quartic flat centre does distinguish the two weights

For \(V=\lambda z^4/4\) and \(\mu=0\), symmetry gives \(\mathbb EZ=0\) and \(z_*=0\). Write

\[g=\frac{w\lambda\hbar}{a^2},\qquad \chi_\hbar=A_\hbar''(0).\tag{15}\]

The classical curvature is \(A_0''(0)=0\). For every supplied \(\hbar>0\), however,

\[ 0<a\frac{\sqrt{1+4g}-1}{\sqrt{1+4g}+1} \le\chi_\hbar\le a\min(1,3g). \tag{16} \]

Moreover \(\chi_\hbar/(ag)\to3\) as \(g\downarrow0\).

Proof. Integration of \((ze^{-F_0/\hbar})'\) gives

\[a\mathbb EZ^2+w\lambda\mathbb EZ^4=\hbar,\qquad \chi_\hbar=\frac{aw\lambda}{\hbar}\mathbb EZ^4.\tag{17}\]

Let \(r=a\mathbb EZ^2/\hbar\). Jensen’s inequality \(\mathbb EZ^4\ge(\mathbb EZ^2)^2\) gives \(r+gr^2\le1\), hence \(r\le2/(1+\sqrt{1+4g})\). Equation (7) gives \(\chi_\hbar=a(1-r)\), proving the lower bound. Equation (6) at \(n=2\) gives \(\mathbb EZ^4\le3(\hbar/a)^2\), proving the upper bound together with \(\chi_\hbar\le a\).

Under \(Z=\sqrt{\hbar/a}\,X\), the normalized density of \(X\) is proportional to \(e^{-X^2/2-gX^4/4}\). Its fourth moment tends to the Gaussian value 3 as \(g\downarrow0\) by dominated convergence. Equation (17) gives the stated limit. \(\square\)

This is a finite nonlinear example in which integrating fluctuations generates a restoring quadratic term at a classically flat point. The local frequency associated with the defined coarse potential is \(\omega_{\mathrm{cell}}^2=\chi_\hbar/(mw)\), so

\[ \omega_{\mathrm{cell}}^2 \sim\frac{3\lambda\hbar}{ma}\quad(g\downarrow0). \tag{18} \]

The word frequency here describes this coarse-potential diagnostic; it is not an asserted spectral gap or a real-time oscillation frequency without a reconstruction argument.

A direct insertion calculation verifies the generated term. At fixed \(a,w,\hbar,\mu\), the right derivative at \(\lambda=0\) is

\[ \left.\partial_\lambda A_\hbar(\mu)\right|_{0+} =\frac w4\left[\mu^4+6\mu^2\frac\hbar a +3\left(\frac\hbar a\right)^2\right]. \tag{19} \]

This follows by differentiating (3) under the Gaussian integral. It is a statement about a derivative, not a claim that the full perturbation series converges. It agrees with the coefficient 3 in (18).

5. One nonlinear halving and its large-field events

In a finite nearest-neighbour Euclidean time chain with fixed endpoint positions, use the normalized joint law and choose a set \(\mathcal I\) of interior vertices with no two adjacent, retaining all other vertices. Conditioned on the retained values, the variables \(Z_j\), \(j\in\mathcal I\), are independent, each with a density of the form (3), with its own \(a_j,w_j,\mu_j\). The exact retained kernel remains a product of nearest-neighbour kernels, although their actions need not have the original kinetic-plus-potential form.

Define a bad event relative to each cell’s conditional minimizing value:

\[ B_j=\left\{|Z_j-z_{*,j}|>b\sqrt{\hbar/a_j}\right\},\qquad p_b=2e^{-b^2/2}. \tag{20} \]

For any \(H\subset\mathcal I\), Proposition 1 and conditional independence give, including after averaging the retained variables,

\[\mathbb P\left(\bigcap_{j\in H}B_j\right)\le p_b^{|H|}. \tag{21}\]

For \(b>\sqrt{2\log2}\) this is a decaying bound. It controls the normalized nonlinear measure, including configurations outside the chosen tubes. It is one halving statement for this convex local action. After further halvings the generated interactions must be controlled; we have not proved that the same original-potential formula closes.

6. Written controls and physical meaning

For \(V=0\), (3) gives \(A_\hbar=0\) and variance \(\hbar/a\), saturating (7). For \(V=kz^2/2\), \(k\ge0\), completing the square gives

\[ A_\hbar(\mu)=\frac{awk}{2(a+wk)}\mu^2 +\frac\hbar2\log\left(1+\frac{wk}{a}\right). \tag{22} \]

Its curvature agrees exactly with (10) and (12); the fluctuation term is independent of \(\mu\) and leaves that curvature unchanged. This quadratic control distinguishes a force-curvature effect from the quartic fluctuation-generated curvature in section 4.

For \(V=-Fz\), (3) gives

\[ A_\hbar=-wF\mu-\frac{w^2F^2}{2a},\qquad \frac{w^2F^2}{2a}=\frac{F^2uvT}{8m}. \tag{23} \]

This recovers the arbitrary-cut Galileo correction in the time-blocking note, with its normalization explicit.

The cell curvature in (16) is a possible scale diagnostic, conditional on the entire weighting model. If \(a,w,\lambda\) are independently fixed and \(0<\chi<a\) is the measured value of the defined cell curvature, inverting (16) gives the model-conditional action interval

\[ \frac{a\chi}{3w\lambda}\le\hbar \le\frac{a\chi}{w\lambda(1-\chi/a)^2}. \tag{24} \]

Indeed, with \(q=\chi/a\), (16) gives \(q/3\le g\) and \(\sqrt{1+4g}\le(1+q)/(1-q)\), so \(g\le q/(1-q)^2\). The lower endpoint is asymptotically sharp in the small-\(g\) limit. This inference presupposes that the measured response is the response of (3), rather than an unmodeled apparatus effect.

A nonzero measured value could also come from a classical statistical weight with an independently supplied action-like noise parameter. ODE regularity or this positive Euclidean integral alone cannot identify that parameter with the real-time phase constant, a universal apparatus restriction, or Planck’s measured value. Those are separate physical tests. The bounded full-cell result is useful because it makes the alternatives quantitatively testable.

7. Source comparison: full densities and locality

Dimock’s exposition concerns a three-dimensional lattice scalar \(\phi^4\) partition function. Its ultraviolet stability is neither a Yang–Mills construction nor a mass-gap theorem. The following are selected passages read, not an audit of the full proofs.

  • Dimock I, §2.1, (15), (26)–(28), integrates against a normalized Gaussian noisy block-average kernel and rescales; (28) preserves the total integral. Conditional elimination of time coordinates here, or the exact Brownian-reference tower \(\rho_n=\mathbb E_0[\rho_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]\), is a different map. A tower identity alone supplies none of his uniform locality estimates.
  • Sections 2.2–2.3 separate background and Gaussian fluctuation. The one-cell analogue retains its minimizing coordinate, covariance and normalization; with interactions it retains the whole conditional law. The quartic derivative
    1. above already displays generated quadratic and constant terms; it is a derivative at zero coupling, not convergence of a perturbation series.
  • Section 4.1, (176)–(180), and Theorem 14 propagate normalization, mass, energy, quartic and polymer-remainder terms under small-coupling and sufficiently large-block assumptions. A finite polynomial ansatz does not retain all of that information. Section 4.5 introduces weakening parameters before clustering.
  • Small-field cutoffs (172)–(175) are only part of the analysis. Dimock II, introduction, §3.3 and Theorem 3.1, sums histories of regions, including large fields and boundaries, with the block-size, mass, running-coupling and Part I counterterm hypotheses. The identity \(\prod_b[\chi_b+(1-\chi_b)]=1\) retains every pattern; its good-region tests involve derivatives and nested regions, not just our midpoint fluctuation. Our normalized tail estimate controls a complement rather than deleting it.
  • Part I §2.4 and Lemma 6 earn Green-function locality through local inverses and a convergent random-walk expansion. Part II §3.13, Lemmas 3.15–3.17, localizes interactions, remainders and boundary terms; §3.14, Lemma 3.18, clusters with holes. Correlated local fluctuations are not automatically independent Gaussian variables. The time chain’s conditional independence holds for one halving and establishes no multiscale polymer estimate in three or four dimensions.

The Bałaban ICM overview, printed pp.1260–1262 (text read), motivates this full-density discipline. The correspondence is about what estimates must be retained; no field-theory proof is transferred to mechanics or conversely.

8. Consequence for STATE

The first nonlinear time-elimination step is now controlled without replacing its law by a Gaussian: normalized tails, endpoint response, coarse curvature and a simultaneous one-halving bad-event bound are explicit. The convex quartic generates positive flat-centre curvature at supplied \(\hbar>0\), whereas classical elimination remains regular with zero flat-centre curvature. Next investigate branch competition or real-time/record reconstruction of this diagnostic. The Newton necessity question remains active; no positivity of \(\hbar\) is derived from the choice of a positive weight. These are written, unrefereed calculations.
