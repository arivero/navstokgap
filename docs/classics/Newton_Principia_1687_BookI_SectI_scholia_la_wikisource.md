## What it supplies

The Latin witness, in this repository, for what Newton wrote in Book I
about division, in the first edition. It answers a gap found by the
adversarial review of 2026-09-23: the paper quoted the Section I scholium
from a sibling repository's passage store, with no witness here.

- **Lemma XI, Corollary 3 (1687 numbering).** The ultimate curvilinear
  areas between tangent and arc are "duæ tertiæ partes triangulorum
  rectilineorum", two thirds of the rectilinear triangles, and the
  segments "partes tertiæ", one third, "ex natura Parabolæ". These are
  the thirds the Planck-gap notes use: the inertial--parabola area is two
  thirds of the tangent triangle, the chord's segment one third.
- **The scholium after Lemma XI, on contact angles.** Series of contact
  angles can be continued without end, and "inter duos quosvis ex his
  angulis potest series utrinq; in infinitum pergens angulorum
  intermediorum inseri"; between any two of the new series another can be
  inserted, "Neq; novit natura limitem": nature knows no limit. Newton's
  own statement, in Book I, that insertion has no end in the geometry.
- **The scholium closing Section I, first paragraph.** The lemmas were put
  first "ut effugerem tædium deducendi perplexas demonstrationes, more
  veterum Geometrarum, ad absurdum": to avoid the tedium of the ancient
  geometers' reductio, the method of exhaustion that Archimedes credits
  to Eudoxus; the method of indivisibles is shorter, "Sed quoniam durior
  est indivisibilium Hypothesis", Newton prefers limits, and where he
  later speaks of particles he means "evanescentia divisibilia".
- **The same scholium, second paragraph.** The objection that vanishing
  quantities have no ultimate ratio, "quippe quæ, antequam evanuerunt,
  non est ultima, ubi evanuerunt, nulla est", and its extension to motion:
  there would be no ultimate velocity of a body reaching a place. Newton's
  answer: the ultimate velocity is "illam ipsam velocitatem quacum corpus
  attingit locum ultimum", the very velocity with which the body reaches
  its last place; "Extat limes quem velocitas in fine motus attingere
  potest, non autem transgredi. Hæc est velocitas ultima", and since "hic
  limes sit certus & definitus", determining it is a truly geometrical
  problem. This is Newton's reply to the instant-versus-interval
  objection of Zeno's arrow: a determinate velocity assigned to arrival
  at a place, which the
  [fifth-postulate note](../../notes/principia-fifth-postulate.md) reads as
  joint determinacy of place and motion.
- **The same scholium, third paragraph.** The objection that given
  ultimate ratios give ultimate magnitudes, so that "quantitas omnis
  constabit ex indivisibilibus, contra quam Euclides de incommensurabilibus,
  in libro decimo Elementorum, demonstravit", is answered by limits, and
  the scholium closes "cave intelligas quantitates magnitudine
  determinatas, sed cogita semper diminuendas sine limite".
