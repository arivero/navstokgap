## What it supplies

**The Buddhist counterpart of Zeno's arrow, argued and accepted rather than
posed as a paradox.** Refuting the view that bodily action is motion,
Vasubandhu writes (ad IV.2, Pradhan 193.1–4):

> *na gatir yasmāt saṃskṛtaṃ kṣaṇikaṃ* — there is no motion, because the
> conditioned is momentary … *sarvaṃ hi saṃskṛtam ātmalābhād ūrdhvaṃ na
> bhavatīti yatraiva jātaṃ tatraiva dhvasyate* — everything conditioned does
> not exist beyond its acquisition of being, so it is destroyed in the very
> place where it arose … *tasyāyuktā deśāntarasaṃkrāntiḥ* — its passage to
> another place is therefore impossible.

The definition of the moment is given in the same breath: *ātmalābho
'nantaravināśī*, acquisition of being that perishes immediately after. Zeno's
arrow concludes that motion is impossible and treats that as absurd; this
concludes the same and accepts it, so that what looks like travel is a
succession of arisings, each in its own place. Against the Vaiśeṣika arrow,
which makes flight *many motions* carried by an impression, this makes it
*no motion at all*. The collection now holds both Indian answers.

**A second use of propagation, inside the atom debate** (ad I.43, Pradhan
33.2–7). Vasumitra: if atoms touched, they would persist into a later moment.
The Bhadanta: they do not touch, and "touching" names the case of no
interval. Vasubandhu's reason for accepting that is a propagation argument —
*anyathā hi sāntarāṇāṃ paramāṇūnāṃ śūnyeṣv antareṣu gatiḥ kena
pratibadhyeta*, otherwise, atoms having intervals, by what would motion
through the empty intervals be obstructed? The passage closes with the
directional-parts reductio: if the atom is given parts by direction, it has
parts, touched or not. That is the Buddhist form of the stopping rule for
division, beside the Nyāya *paramāṇu* and Democritus's cone.
