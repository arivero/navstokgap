\section{Constructing Newton motion: refinement, continuation and
regularity}\label{constructing-newton-motion-refinement-continuation-and-regularity}

\textbf{Maintained result.} For a globally Lipschitz acceleration,
arbitrary positive partitions and independently mixed kick--drift orders
construct the same unique initial-value motion, with an explicit error
proportional to the mesh. A conservative logarithmic force family shows
the exact limit of that sufficient hypothesis: an unbounded slope still
permits unique motion when \(0<\beta\le2\); finite-time arrival and
waiting branches occur for \(\beta>2\). Local action tends to zero in
both regimes. These are classical existence and uniqueness statements,
with no positive action premise.

For a continuous outward central force, the departure of one moving
orbit from its inertial tangent at successive half-times determines
whether a body initially at rest at the centre can leave. The short-time
force coefficient converges uniformly over bounded-speed orbits in both
cases. Allowing the force to change sign gives two explicit laws with
the same moving positions, velocities and accelerations at all those
times and opposite answers about rest.

At a point of nonzero speed and curvature, one finite inward force gives
opposite continuum orbits for the two orders of an impulse and a free
flight. A nonnegative spherical gravitating source restores unique
nonradial motion. For the branching force, uniform agreement of the
canonical products over a set of trial actions is equivalent to a
positive infimum of that set. A positive lower width for classical
ensembles gives the same refinement property.

The polygon proof uses standard stability, consistency and Gronwall
arguments. The arrival test is a direct energy calculation, not the
general first-order Osgood criterion. Teschl {[}@TeschlODE2012, §2.7,
pp.54--56 and Problem 2.25, p.58; passages read{]} supplies the Euler
compactness background; López Pouso {[}@LopezPouso2005; metadata and
abstract read{]} treats a broader second-order problem whose full
theorem is not used here. See the
\href{../docs/Teschl_ODE_2012.md}{source companion}. Internal Terra/Sol
checks of the written arguments were model reviews, not independent peer
review or novelty evidence.

\subsection{1. Start with a finite Newton
polygon}\label{start-with-a-finite-newton-polygon}

Let \(m>0\) be the mass and \(f(q)=F(q)/m\) the acceleration. The
desired motion would satisfy
\[\dot q=v,\qquad\dot v=f(q),\qquad(q(0),v(0))=(q_0,v_0).\tag{1}\]

For now we do not assume that a solution exists. Given a positive time
step \(\tau\), define a drift and a force kick by \[
 D_\tau(q,v)=(q+\tau v,v),\qquad
 K_\tau(q,v)=(q,v+\tau f(q)).
 \tag{2}
\]

A step can kick first, \(D_\tau K_\tau\), or drift first,
\(K_\tau D_\tau\). The first choice uses the force at the old position;
the second uses the force at the new position. Both give a finite
piecewise straight trajectory with impulses. Neither is assumed to agree
exactly with the unknown continuous motion.

Fix \(T>0\), take any finite positive partition of \([0,T]\), choose
either ordering in each cell, and compose the maps chronologically.
There is no equal-step or nesting requirement. When a time is inserted,
recompute the later states from the fixed initial state and the
prescribed force rule.

Existence of the limit requires two kinds of estimates. Stability bounds
how differences grow. Consistency bounds the error in the finite force
balance. The two together will construct the ODE solution.

\subsection{2. Lipschitz control supplies
stability}\label{lipschitz-control-supplies-stability}

Assume for this construction that
\[|f(q)-f(Q)|\le\Lambda|q-Q|\quad(q,Q\in\mathbb R),\qquad\Lambda\ge0.
\tag{3}\]

The quantity \(\Lambda\) has units of inverse time squared. Choose a
reference frequency \(\rho>0\) and use the length-valued phase-space
norm \[
 \|(q,v)\|_\rho=|q|+\frac{|v|}{\rho},\qquad
 \alpha=\max(\rho,\Lambda/\rho),\quad
 \beta=\rho+\Lambda/\rho,\quad b=|f(0)|/\rho.
 \tag{4}
\]

There is no action constant in these definitions. Directly from (2), \[
 \operatorname{Lip}(D_\tau)\le1+\rho\tau,\qquad
 \operatorname{Lip}(K_\tau)\le1+\Lambda\tau/\rho.
 \tag{5}
\]

Thus either cell map has Lipschitz constant at most \(e^{\beta\tau}\).
The constants multiply under composition, and their exponents add. Every
finite polygon therefore has the same upper amplification
\(e^{\beta T}\), independently of the number and placement of vertices.

We also need a bound on the states themselves, because consistency
involves the force along the polygon. For either cell map \(M_\tau\), \[
 \|M_\tau z\|_\rho\le e^{\beta\tau}\|z\|_\rho
                         +\tau e^{\beta\tau}b.
 \tag{6}
\]

For a kick-first cell, the inhomogeneous term is actually at most
\(\tau(1+\rho\tau)b\); for a drift-first cell it is \(\tau b\).
Iterating (6) gives the common vertex bound
\[R=e^{\beta T}(\|z_0\|_\rho+Tb).\tag{7}\]

In particular \(|q_j|\le R\), \(|v_j|\le\rho R\), and
\(|f(q_j)|\le A:=|f(0)|+\Lambda R\). A very irregular partition has not
created a new force or stability constant.

\subsection{3. The polygons construct the ODE
solution}\label{the-polygons-construct-the-ode-solution}

Linearly interpolate both vertex coordinates to obtain
\(z_\pi(t)=(q_\pi(t),v_\pi(t))\). The displayed velocity is continuous;
it differs from the physical constant velocity along each straight
polygon segment by at most that cell's kick. This distinction disappears
with the mesh.

Let \(\delta=|\pi|\) be the maximum cell duration. In a kick-first cell,
with elapsed time \(s\in[0,\tau]\), \[
 \dot q_\pi-v_\pi=(\tau-s)f(q_j),\qquad
 \dot v_\pi-f(q_\pi)=f(q_j)-f(q_\pi).
 \tag{8}
\]

In a drift-first cell the analogous expressions are \(-sf(q_{j+1})\) and
\(f(q_{j+1})-f(q_\pi)\). The bounds in (7) give, for almost every time,
\[
 \|\dot z_\pi-\mathcal F(z_\pi)\|_\rho\le C\delta,
 \quad\mathcal F(q,v)=(v,f(q)),\quad C=|f(0)|+2\Lambda R.
 \tag{9}
\]

The vector field \(\mathcal F\) is Lipschitz with constant \(\alpha\) in
the norm (4). Two polygons with the same initial state consequently
satisfy \[
 \sup_{t\le T}\|z_\pi(t)-z_\sigma(t)\|_\rho
 \le C\frac{e^{\alpha T}-1}{\alpha}
                        (|\pi|+|\sigma|).
 \tag{10}
\]

To verify this estimate, integrate the two residual equations (9),
subtract, and apply the elementary integral Gronwall inequality. That
inequality follows by integrating the differential inequality for an
upper bound after multiplying by \(e^{-\alpha t}\).

Completeness of continuous path space now constructs a uniform limit
\(z(t)\). The residual integral tends to zero; the Lipschitz bound
passes \(\mathcal F(z_\pi)\) to \(\mathcal F(z)\) under the time
integral. Hence \[z(t)=z_0+\int_0^t\mathcal F(z(s))\,ds.\tag{11}\]

The limit is differentiable and solves (1). Applying the same difference
estimate with zero residual proves uniqueness. Letting one mesh tend to
zero in (10) gives a quantitative error for any other partition. Both
kick orders, mixed arbitrarily, lead to the same solution. Since \(T\)
was arbitrary, the global Lipschitz hypothesis constructs forward motion
for every finite time.

This is the first analogy with Balaban: one proves bounds for each
finite description before appealing to a limiting object. The limit
follows from estimates uniform in the refinement. Nothing in this
argument requires choosing \(\hbar>0\).

\subsection{4. Local continuation and velocity-dependent
forces}\label{local-continuation-and-velocity-dependent-forces}

For locally Lipschitz acceleration, extend it from a bounded region and
apply the construction until the trajectory leaves that region.
Continuation then requires bounds preventing escape from every compact
phase-space region.

For \(V\in C^1\) with locally Lipschitz \(\nabla V\), (1) has a unique
local solution, by the usual Cauchy--Lipschitz theorem. Along it,
\[E(q,v)=\tfrac12m|v|^2+V(q),\qquad \frac d{dt}E=0.\] If \(V\) is
bounded below, (2) bounds \(|v|\). On any finite interval
\(|q(t)|\le |q_0|+|t|\sup|v|\), so \((q,v)\) remains in a compact set.
The local continuation theorem then gives a unique solution for all
positive and negative times. Coercivity of \(V\) is a stronger condition
than is needed for this finite-time argument.

Smoothness alone does not guarantee global existence. For \(m=1\),
\(V(q)=-q^4/4\), \(q_0>0\) and \(v_0=q_0^2/\sqrt2\), the zero-energy
solution \[q(t)=\frac{q_0}{1-q_0t/\sqrt2}\] escapes to infinity at
\(t=\sqrt2/q_0\). A force singularity can also stop a classical
solution: for example, a radial attractive Kepler trajectory can reach
\(r=0\) in finite time. A rule for continuing through the singular point
is further data.

In three dimensions the Lorentz force, with a static scalar potential
and a static magnetic field, gives \[
 \dot q=v,\qquad m\dot v=-\nabla V(q)+e\,v\times B(q).
\] It is not a force depending on position alone. If \(\nabla V\) and
\(B\) are defined and locally Lipschitz on all of \(\mathbb R^3\), the
full phase-space vector field is locally Lipschitz, so local existence
and uniqueness still hold. Moreover \(v\cdot(v\times B)=0\), hence the
conservative energy is conserved. If \(V\) is bounded below, the same
speed and position bounds as above prove global existence and
uniqueness, even if \(B\) is not globally bounded.

For instance, if \(V\) is constant and \(B=B_0\hat z\), the field
rotates the transverse velocity with cyclotron frequency \(eB_0/m\) and
leaves the longitudinal velocity constant. A velocity-dependent force is
thus not an exception to the ODE theory; its dependence must be included
in the phase-space vector field being estimated.

If \(B=\nabla\times A\) on \(\mathbb R^3\), the quantum Hamiltonian is
\[H_{\hbar,A}=\frac1{2m}(-i\hbar\nabla-eA(q))^2+V(q).\] The kinetic
momenta generally do not commute:
\([\pi_i,\pi_j]=i\hbar e(\partial_iA_j-\partial_jA_i)\). A positive
scalar Brownian-bridge density does not directly cover this magnetic
problem: a magnetic Euclidean representation contains a phase. The
\href{quantum-blocks-construct-newton.md}{coherent-packet theorem}
treats the scalar Hamiltonian; it does not prove this magnetic
extension.

An endpoint problem fixes \(q(0)\) and \(q(T)\) rather than initial
velocity. It has a separate possible singularity. For the harmonic
oscillator the full phase-space derivative is \[
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \cos\omega T&\sin(\omega T)/\omega\\
 -\omega\sin(\omega T)&\cos\omega T
 \end{pmatrix},\qquad \det=1.
\]

The position response to initial velocity is \(\sin(\omega T)/\omega\).
It vanishes at a conjugate time while the full phase-space map stays
invertible. The corresponding endpoint action Hessian can fail to invert
even though the initial-value motion remains unique. The time-blocking
note gives a finite-polygon determinant that approaches this singularity
under refinement.

Therefore a failure of one position-only generating function is not, by
itself, a failure of Newton's dynamics. A physical completion argument
has to establish which extra information the complete theory needs and
why it cannot be retained classically.

\subsection{5. The conservative arrival
criterion}\label{the-conservative-arrival-criterion}

Near zero let \(a\) be continuous, odd, positive on \((0,r_0)\), zero at
zero and smooth away from zero. Consider \[
 \ddot q=a(q),\qquad W(q)=\int_0^q a(s)\,ds\quad(q\ge0),
 \qquad V(q)=-mW(|q|),\quad m>0.
 \tag{L1}
\]

Assume a bounded continuous global extension of the force, smooth away
from zero, by cutting off the potential outside a neighborhood of zero.
The \href{newton-cusp-preparation-floor.md}{bounded-cusp argument},
section 1, then gives global existence and bounds preventing escape. The
issue remaining here is uniqueness, not existence.

Conservation of \(E=m\dot q^2/2+V(q)\) follows by the chain rule since
\(V\in C^1\). Any solution starting at rest at zero has \(E=0\). On a
positive departing segment its velocity must be \[
 \dot q=\sqrt{2W(q)},\qquad
 \mathcal T(q)=\int_0^q\frac{ds}{\sqrt{2W(s)}}.
 \tag{L2}
\]

\textbf{Arrival criterion.} If \(\mathcal T(q)=\infty\) for one, hence
every small \(q>0\), rest is the unique local solution from \((0,0)\).
If \(\mathcal T(q)<\infty\), the inverse \(Q=\mathcal T^{-1}\)
constructs departures \(q(t)=\pm Q(t-t_0)\) after every waiting time
\(t_0\).

For the first implication, a nonconstant solution has an excursion of
one sign starting from zero. Its velocity cannot vanish inside that
excursion because \(W>0\), and separation of variables would give a
finite value for the divergent integral (L2). For the converse,
\(Q'=\sqrt{2W(Q)}\) and \(Q''=a(Q)\). Both tend to zero at departure, so
adjoining rest gives a \(C^2\) Newton trajectory. Reversing the two
departures supplies two different incoming full states that merge at
zero in finite time, exactly as in the cusp note.

For \(a(q)=c q^\alpha\) this test reproduces the familiar \(\alpha<1\)
nonuniqueness threshold: its integrand is proportional to
\(q^{-(1+\alpha)/2}\). The logarithmic family below tests the more
delicate border where no power-law Lipschitz constant exists.

\subsubsection{Exact logarithmic
threshold}\label{exact-logarithmic-threshold}

Fix \(\Omega,\ell>0\), \(\beta>0\) and put \(r=\beta/2\). For
sufficiently small \(q>0\), with \(L=\log(\ell/q)>\max\{1,r\}\), set \[
 W(q)=\frac{\Omega^2}{2}q^2 L^\beta,\qquad
 a(q)=\Omega^2q\bigl(L^\beta-rL^{\beta-1}\bigr).
 \tag{L3}
\]

Extend \(a\) oddly near zero and the potential with a smooth cutoff
farther out. This gives a bounded \(C^1\) potential and continuous
bounded force; \(a(0)=0\) since \(q L^\beta\to0\). Its derivative on the
positive side is \[
 a'(q)=\Omega^2\left[L^\beta-\frac{3\beta}{2}L^{\beta-1}
              +\frac{\beta(\beta-1)}2L^{\beta-2}\right],
 \tag{L4}
\]

which diverges for every \(\beta>0\). Thus none of these forces is
locally Lipschitz at zero.

Nevertheless the energy-zero speed and arrival integral are exact: \[
 \dot q=\Omega qL^r,\qquad
 \mathcal T(q)=\frac1\Omega\int_L^\infty u^{-r}\,du.
 \tag{L5}
\]

Consequently rest is unique for \(\beta\le2\). For \(\beta>2\), \[
 \mathcal T(q)=\frac{L^{1-r}}{\Omega(r-1)},\qquad
 Q(t)=\ell\exp\left(-[\Omega(r-1)t]^{-1/(r-1)}\right).
 \tag{L6}
\]

The formula for \(Q\) is valid until the departure leaves the region
where (L3) is used. It is flat to every order at \(t=0\), so an exactly
resting body and one that leaves after a delay satisfy the same Newton
equation and initial data.

For \(\beta\le2\) the \emph{whole} extended IVP is unique. Away from
zero this is ordinary local Lipschitz uniqueness. At zero with nonzero
initial velocity, the conserved-energy first-order equation has a
nonzero square root and a \(C^1\) right-hand side near the crossing, so
it gives unique passage. At zero velocity the arrival criterion applies.
Bounded force then gives a global reversible point flow. Its dependence
on initial state is continuous: common finite-time bounds give
compactness for any converging initial sequence; every subsequential
limit solves the same unique IVP. Thus all subsequences have the same
limit. A divergent local slope has not destroyed reversible mechanics.

At the borderline \(\beta=2\), the outgoing energy-zero solution from
\(q_0>0\) is particularly informative: \[
 q(t)=\ell\left(\frac{q_0}{\ell}\right)^{e^{-\Omega t}},
 \qquad \dot q(0)=\Omega q_0\log(\ell/q_0).
 \tag{L7}
\]

While it remains in the logarithmic region, it stays continuous as
\(q_0\downarrow0\) at every fixed finite time, although its position
sensitivity diverges. This is a genuine example of unique stable motion
without a Lipschitz dependence bound at rest.

The general first-order Osgood sufficient test applied directly to a
modulus of size \(s[\log(1/s)]^\beta\) reaches only \(\beta\le1\). The
stronger range \(1<\beta\le2\) above uses the conservative Newton
structure and energy; failure of that first-order sufficient test is not
a proof of nonuniqueness.

\subsubsection{What one moving orbit determines about
rest}\label{what-one-moving-orbit-determines-about-rest}

\textbf{6.1 Sol Max, 2026-11-18.} In the 1713 \emph{Principia}, Lemma X,
Corollary 4 relates initial displacement divided by time squared to
force. Proposition VI invokes it for the departure from an orbital
tangent. The following is a modern question about the same quadratic
coefficient. Can it converge uniformly under refinement even though a
body at rest has several continuations? We use a diameter orbit.
Proposition VI's geometric ratios are defined at positive radius.
\href{https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/diplomatic/NATP00082}{Newton,
1713, Book I} (Lemma X and Proposition VI, passages).

Write the force as \(m\mathbf a(q)\), with \(m>0\). On a compact region
\(K\) where \(\mathbf a\) is continuous, let \(\omega_K\) be its modulus
of continuity. Every exact orbit staying in \(K\) at speed at most \(V\)
obeys

\[
 \left|\frac{2[q(t+s)-q(t)-s\dot q(t)]}{s^2}
                 -\mathbf a(q(t))\right|\le \omega_K(Vs). \tag{D1}
\]

The quotient averages the acceleration with the unit-mass weight
\(2(s-u)/s^2\) on \(0\le u\le s\). This is the integral form of Newton's
equation in (1). Thus even for a family of trajectories the coefficient
converges uniformly, with the same bound on every inserted cell of
duration \(s\).

Now take \(\mathbf a(q)=a(r)q/r\) for \(0<r\le r_0\) and
\(\mathbf a(0)=0\), where \(a\) is continuous, \(a(0)=0\), \(a\ge0\),
and \(W(r)=\int_0^r a(u)\,du>0\) for \(r>0\). The magnitude \(a\) may
oscillate. Start a radial solution \(x_v\) at the centre with any speed
\(v>0\). Its radius increases uniquely by
\(\dot x_v=\sqrt{v^2+2W(x_v)}\). Take \(H>0\) small enough that
\(x_v(H)\le r_0\) and \(\dot x_v\le 2v\). Define the dimensionless
excess over the inertial tangent and a sum with units of time by

\[
 d(s)=\frac{x_v(s)-vs}{vs},\qquad
 t_n=2^{-n}H,\qquad
 S_v(H)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{t_n}{\sqrt{d(t_n)}}. \tag{D2}
\]

\textbf{Criterion.} Rest at the centre is the unique forward motion
locally if \(S_v(H)=\infty\). If \(S_v(H)<\infty\), a body can wait for
any time and then leave along any ray. More precisely, the zero-energy
travel time \(\mathcal T\) in (L2) obeys

\[
 \frac{S_v(H)}8\le \mathcal T(x_v(H))
            \le\frac{2}{\sqrt3} S_v(H). \tag{D3}
\]

The rest criterion in (L2) is the usual conservative quadrature. Angular
momentum from rest is zero, so every departing excursion lies on a ray.
Comparison (D3) uses positions of the moving orbit alone, with no
differentiation of its tangent excess.
\href{https://doi.org/10.1112/S0024610705006319}{López Pouso, 2005}
(abstract, general scalar existence criterion).

To prove (D3), put \(z(s)=\dot x_v(s)/v-1\). Outward acceleration gives
nondecreasing \(0\le z\le1\). Its average \(d(s)\) satisfies
\(z(s/2)/2\le d(s)\le z(s)\) and is nondecreasing. Changing variables
\(r=x_v(s)\) in (L2) gives

\[
 \mathcal T(x_v(H))
   =\int_0^H\frac{1+z(s)}{\sqrt{z(s)[2+z(s)]}}\,ds .
\]

For \(0\le z\le1\) the factor \((1+z)/\sqrt{2+z}\) lies between
\(1/\sqrt2\) and \(2/\sqrt3\). If \(I_d=\int_0^H ds/\sqrt{d(s)}\) and
\(I_z=\int_0^H ds/\sqrt{z(s)}\), the average bounds give
\(I_z\le I_d\le2\sqrt2 I_z\). On \([t_{n+1},t_n]\), the endpoint values
of \(1/\sqrt d\) bracket the integrand. Summing the two bounds gives
\(S_v(H)/2\le I_d\le S_v(H)\) and proves (D3), including the divergent
case. The improper integral also equals the supremum of lower sums on
compact subintervals of \((0,H]\). Its finiteness is independent of
choosing dyadic subdivisions and of the nonzero speed chosen in (D2).

For the force in (L3) let \(L_s=\log(\ell/(vs))\). Since
\(x_v(s)/(vs)\to1\), energy gives \(z(s)\sim\Omega^2s^2 L_s^\beta/2\).
Averaging gives \(d(s)\sim\Omega^2s^2 L_s^\beta/6\), using
\(\int_0^s u^2 L_u^\beta du\sim s^3 L_s^\beta/3\). The terms of (D2) are
therefore asymptotic to \(\sqrt6/(\Omega[L_H+n\log2]^{\beta/2})\). Every
member of the family has the uniform finite-force limit (D1). The sum
diverges for \(0<\beta\le2\) and converges at every \(\beta>2\), where
even an arbitrarily delayed departure obeys that same limit. Here
\(r_0,\ell\) are lengths, \(H\) is a time, \(v,V\) are speeds and
\(\Omega\) is an inverse time. Both outcomes occur in classical
mechanics with no action parameter.

\subsubsection{Two central forces with the same positions at successive
half-times}\label{two-central-forces-with-the-same-positions-at-successive-half-times}

Keep the work primitive \(W(r)>0\) for \(r>0\) and allow the radial
acceleration to reverse sign. The positions, velocities and
accelerations of a moving solution at the whole sequence \(t_n=2^{-n}H\)
can then agree for two force laws whose solutions from rest have
different uniqueness properties. This pair uses autonomous potentials
fixed across all \(n\).

Fix \(H,v,m>0\). Prescribe two increasing radius functions by
\(x_i(t)=v\int_0^t(1+z_i(s))\,ds\) on \([0,H]\), starting with
\(z_1(t)=(t/H)^{3/2}\). In each \(I_n=[t_n/2,t_n]\) set \(u=2t/t_n-1\).
Use the smooth step \(g(s)=\rho(s)/(\rho(s)+\rho(1-s))\), where
\(\rho(s)=e^{-1/s}\) for \(s>0\) and is zero otherwise. Put
\(\chi(u)=g(8u-1)g(5-8u)\) and \(\psi(u)=8g'(8u-6)\). Then
\(0\le\chi\le1\) is one on \([1/4,1/2]\), while the nonnegative \(\psi\)
has disjoint support and integrates to one. Set

\[
 \begin{split}
 b_n&=\frac{2}{t_n}\int_{I_n}\chi(2s/t_n-1)
                     [z_1(s)-(s/H)^2]\,ds,\\
 z_2(t)&=(1-\chi(u))z_1(t)+\chi(u)(t/H)^2+b_n\psi(u).
 \end{split} \tag{D4}
\]

The subtracted and added integrals are equal on each \(I_n\). Hence
\(x_1(t_n)=x_2(t_n)\) by summing the intervals down to zero. Both
\(z_i\) coincide in a neighbourhood of each node, so the speeds and
accelerations there also agree. Inside the interval,
\(0\le b_n\le(t_n/H)^{3/2}\) and \(z_2>0\). Differentiation gives
\(|z_2'|\le C(t_n/H)^{1/2}/H\) with
\(C=2(1+\|\chi'\|_\infty+\|\psi'\|_\infty)\). Both \(z_i\) thus extend
to \(C^1\) functions with value and derivative zero at \(t=0\).

Since \(x_i'\ge v\), the assignments

\[
   a_i(x_i(t))=v z_i'(t),\qquad
   W_i(x_i(t))=v^2 z_i(t)(1+z_i(t)/2)
\]

define continuous accelerations with \(a_i(0)=0\) on the same radial
interval. The second identity follows from \(W_i'=a_i\) and
\(W_i(0)=0\). The potentials \(-mW_i(|q|)\) are \(C^1\) at the origin.
They admit bounded-force extensions past the common outer radius. The
first force points outward. The second must turn inward on each
sufficiently small cell to reach \((t/H)^2\) from the larger
\(z_1(t_n/2)\), then turns outward again.

For the first force (L2) is finite since \(\int_0^H(t/H)^{-3/4}dt\) is
finite. For the second, on the plateau \(1/4\le u\le1/2\) one has
\(1/\sqrt{z_2(t)}=H/t\). Its integral contributes at least \(H/8\) on
each cell. The travel-time integrand in the proof of (D3) is at least
\(1/\sqrt{2z_2}\), so (L2) diverges. Thus the first force admits
arbitrary waiting followed by departure, while the second has unique
rest. Inserting a time inside a modified cell can distinguish the two
moving curves. Knowing the complete moving curve even determines
\(W(x_v(t))=(\dot x_v(t)^2-v^2)/2\). Equality here concerns one
particular nested sequence accumulating at the initial instant.

\subsubsection{Crossing times and arbitrary-mesh
polygons}\label{crossing-times-and-arbitrary-mesh-polygons}

Let \(q_n=\ell e^{-n}\) with \(n\) sufficiently large. Along the
zero-energy trajectory, the time for the annulus \(q_{n+1}<q<q_n\) is \[
 \tau_n=\frac1\Omega\int_n^{n+1}u^{-r}\,du,
 \qquad \tau_n\sim\frac1{\Omega n^r}.
 \tag{L8}
\]

On this annulus the local acceleration slope bound is of order
\(\Lambda_n=\Omega^2n^\beta\). The short-cell scale \(\Lambda_n^{-1/2}\)
is therefore of the same order as \(\tau_n\). One can subdivide an
annulus crossing into a fixed number of shorter steps to obtain a small
local Lipschitz amplification parameter. There is no uniform slope bound
across all annuli.

The issue is whether infinitely many controlled annulus crossings can
fit into finite physical time: \[
 \sum_{n\ge N}\tau_n
 \begin{cases}
 =\infty,&\beta\le2,\\
 <\infty,&\beta>2.
 \end{cases}
 \tag{L9}
\]

For \(\beta\le2\), a zero-energy trajectory cannot reach the
infinite-depth boundary at zero in finite time. For \(\beta>2\),
perfectly regular motion on each finite collection of annuli reaches
that boundary in finite time, and the branch datum is missing there.
Local estimates at every finite scale do not by themselves control their
infinite composition. This is the useful multiscale connection with the
Balaban lesson; it is not a transfer of his field-theory proof.

For the unique regime, arbitrary-mesh Euler polygons, and the lesson's
mixed kick--drift polygons, converge uniformly on every fixed finite
time interval. Bounded force gives common position and momentum bounds;
uniform continuity gives a vanishing integral residual; compactness
supplies solution subsequences; uniqueness makes the whole family
converge. No \(O(\text{mesh})\) rate is asserted here.

In the nonunique regime, exact zero initial data make these
deterministic polygons remain at rest on every partition. However,
preparations \(q_0\downarrow0\), \(\dot q_0=\Omega q_0L_0^r\downarrow0\)
have \[
 L(t)=\left[L_0^{1-r}+\Omega(r-1)t\right]^{-1/(r-1)}
       \longrightarrow [\Omega(r-1)t]^{-1/(r-1)}.
 \tag{L10}
\]

Thus they converge to the nonzero departure (L6) at fixed small \(t>0\).
For each positive \(q_0\), choose a fine enough polygon to approximate
that regular trajectory; a diagonal sequence then has vanishing mesh and
vanishing initial error but a departing limit. Refinement is not jointly
stable with preparation refinement at zero. This is a stronger failure
than an unbounded numerical error estimate, while keeping the exact-zero
polygon's resting limit explicit.

\subsubsection{Vanishing local action and a quantum
residual}\label{vanishing-local-action-and-a-quantum-residual}

The positive outgoing momentum is \(p(q)=m\Omega qL^r\). Its canonical
action from zero to \(q\) is \[
 \mathcal J(q)=\int_0^q p(s)\,ds
       =m\Omega\ell^2\int_L^\infty e^{-2u}u^r\,du.
 \tag{L11}
\]

For \(L>r/2\), elementary bounds give \[
 \frac{m\Omega}2q^2L^r
 \le\mathcal J(q)
 \le\frac{m\Omega q^2L^r}{2-r/L}.
 \tag{L12}
\]

The lower bound uses \(u^r\ge L^r\); the upper bound uses
\((L+v)^r\le L^r e^{rv/L}\) after writing \(u=L+v\). Therefore
\(\mathcal J(q)\sim(m\Omega/2)q^2L^r\to0\) for every \(\beta>0\). The
temporal threshold (L9) creates no positive action threshold.

There is still a concrete quantum comparison. In the local region
\(V=-p(q)^2/(2m)\), take the stationary energy-zero test functions \[
 \psi_\pm(q)=p(q)^{-1/2}
          \exp\left(\pm\frac{i}{\hbar}\mathcal J(q)\right),
 \qquad H_\hbar=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dq^2}+V(q).
 \tag{L13}
\]

Their eikonal and first-derivative transport terms cancel exactly.
Writing \(b(q)=p(q)^{-1/2}\) gives \(H_\hbar\psi_\pm
=-(\hbar^2/2m)b''e^{\pm i\mathcal J/\hbar}\) and the exact local ratio
\[
 \frac{|H_\hbar\psi_\pm|}{|V\psi_\pm|}
 =\left[\frac{\hbar}{m\Omega q^2L^r}\right]^2
       \left|\frac34-\frac rL+\frac{r(r+2)}{4L^2}\right|
 \sim\frac{3\hbar^2}{16\mathcal J(q)^2}.
 \tag{L14}
\]

For example, the differentiation behind this identity is
\(p'/p=(1-r/L)/q\) and \(p''/p=-r[1-(r-1)/L]/(q^2L)\); then
\(b''/b=3(p'/p)^2/4-p''/(2p)\). Equation (L14) is a local residual
identity, not an error bound for a normalized state or the full
propagator.

At fixed \(\hbar>0\), action comparable with \(\hbar\) makes the local
residual of this trajectory ansatz order one even in the classically
unique regime. Every sufficiently small positive \(\hbar\) defines a
radius \(q_\hbar\) by \(\mathcal J(q_\hbar)=\hbar\), and that radius
tends to zero as \(\hbar\to0\). The bounded-potential Hamiltonian has
global unitary dynamics at each positive \(\hbar\) by the cusp note's
held Kato--Rellich input. These facts neither require \(\hbar=0\) to be
a member of the same state theory nor select a positive numerical lower
bound across the family. No relativistic ingredient or \(c\) is used.

\subsubsection{A circle with two tangent
departures}\label{a-circle-with-two-tangent-departures}

Use length \(R>0\), inverse time \(\omega>0\) and inertial mass \(m>0\).
In this section positions are in units \(R\), times in \(1/\omega\),
momenta in \(mR\omega\) and energies in \(mR^2\omega^2\). Write
\(r=|q|\). Launch in the plane at \(q_0=(1,0)\) with \(v_0=(0,1)\).
Specific angular momentum in these units is one. The acceleration is
\(-g(r)q/r\). Fix \(b=e^{-20}\) and on \(|y|=|r-1|\le b\) set

\[
 g(r)=r^{-3}-\alpha(y),\qquad
 \alpha(y)=y(L^4-2L^3),\quad L=\log(1/|y|),\qquad \alpha(0)=0. \tag{C1}
\]

The odd \(\alpha\) is strictly increasing there since its derivative off
zero is \(L^2(L^2-6L+6)>0\). Also \(\alpha(b)<1/4\). Hence \(g\) is
positive and strictly decreasing on the annulus. Extend \(g\) linearly
from zero to \(g(1-b)\) on the inside and by \(g(1+b)(1+b)^2/r^2\)
outside. This is one continuous inward force, bounded by \(3\) in
acceleration units, with a bounded \(C^1\) potential \(U\), \(U'=g\).
Its magnitude on the departing outward branch decreases continuously
near the join.

The same force occurs as a reciprocal interaction in a closed Newtonian
two-body Hamiltonian. Take positive inertial masses \(m_1,m_2\) with
reduced mass \(m\) and pair potential \(mR^2\omega^2 U(|q_1-q_2|/R)\) in
dimensional positions. The centre of mass moves freely and the relative
motion is exactly (C1).

The radial equation and angular velocity are \(y''=\alpha(y)\) and
\(\theta'=r^{-2}\). At the common energy, \(|y'|=|y|L^2\) once
\(y\ne0\). Besides the circle, either radial branch can leave after any
waiting time \(s\). Their radii for \(t>s\) are

\[
 r(t)=1\pm Y(t-s),\qquad Y(u)=e^{-1/u}\quad(u>0), \tag{C2}
\]

until \(Y=b\). This is the logarithmic example (L3) translated to a
circular orbit by the centrifugal term. Integrating \(r^{-2}\) gives the
angle with the common value at \(s\). All time derivatives of the two
motions agree with the circle at the join. In physical units the speed
is \(R\omega\), the acceleration magnitude is \(R\omega^2\) and the
curvature is \(1/R\) there. The same entire incoming arc, angular
momentum, energy and short-time force coefficient admit both futures.

\subsubsection{Which orbit the polygons
select}\label{which-orbit-the-polygons-select}

Fix \(0<T<1/40\) in the same time units and set \(h=T/N\). Use exactly
the drift and kick of (2), with the same force and initial point in each
run. Drifting first converges to \(r=1+Y(t)\). Kicking first converges
to \(r=1-Y(t)\) on \([0,T]\). The order difference survives in a
position at a fixed positive time.

For either constant ordering the vertices satisfy
\(q_{n+1}-2q_n+q_{n-1}=-h^2g(r_n)q_n/r_n\) at interior vertices and
\(q_n\wedge q_{n+1}=h\). Here the wedge is the signed planar
determinant. The drift-first first radius is \(\sqrt{1+h^2}\) and the
kick-first one is \(\sqrt{1-h^2+h^4}\). Write \(s_n=\pm(r_n-1)\), with
the plus sign for drift first. In both cases \(s_0=0\) and
\(s_1\ge h^2/4\).

Velocities have norm at most \(1+3T<2\) and the broken lines stay within
\(2T\) of \(q_0\). Compactness and the integral-equation residual from
§3, using the force's uniform continuity there, make every subsequential
limit an exact solution. Such a solution needs at least
\(\int_0^b du/[u\log^2(1/u)]=1/20\) to leave the annulus, by the radial
energy integral. Thus all vertices lie in its interior for large \(N\).
Compactness alone has yet to choose a branch.

Let \(z=h^2/r_n^2\) and \(B(w,z)=(w+\sqrt{w^2-z})^{-1}\). The radial
projection of \(q_{n\pm1}\) on \(q_n/r_n\) is \(\sqrt{r_{n\pm1}^2-z}\)
by the determinant identity. The positive root applies throughout the
ball about \(q_0\). Therefore

\[
 \frac{r_{n+1}-2r_n+r_{n-1}}{h^2}
 =-g(r_n)+\frac{B(r_{n+1},z)+B(r_{n-1},z)}{r_n^2}
 =\alpha(r_n-1)+e_n,\qquad |e_n|\le350h^2 . \tag{C3}
\]

For the constant, each neighbour differs in radius by at most \(2h\).
Taylor's formula for \(|q|\), with Hessian norm at most \(2\) in the
ball, bounds the radial second difference by \((3+8)h^2\). For
\(w\ge1/2\), \(|B(w,z)-1/(2w)|\le z/(2w^3)\le16h^2\). The first two
derivatives of \(1/(2w)\) have magnitude at most \(2\) and \(8\).
Summing its two Taylor expansions about \(r_n\) costs at most
\((22+32)h^2\). Multiplication by \(r_n^{-2}\le4\) gives (C3).

Since \(\alpha(h^2/4)/h^2\to\infty\), induction in (C3) gives positive
increments of \(s_n\) and second difference at least
\(h^2\alpha(s_n)/2\). From level \(w\), reaching \(2w\) costs at most
\(2\sqrt{w/\alpha(w)}+h\) in time, by summing this lower acceleration
until the level is reached. For a fixed small \(0<d<b/2\) use levels
\(w=d2^{-k}\). The mesh contribution to the time to \(d\) is at most
\(h[2+\log_2(4d/h^2)]\) for sufficiently small \(h\). The rest is at
most

\[
 2\sqrt2\sum_{k\ge1}[\log(1/d)+k\log2]^{-2}
 \le \frac{2\sqrt2}{\log2\,\log(1/d)} . \tag{C4}
\]

For every \(t>0\) first choose \(d\) so the latter bound is less than
\(t\), then take \(h\) small. The signed radius has reached \(d\) by
\(t\). Every limit therefore departs immediately on its prescribed side.
The energy integral gives precisely (C2) with \(s=0\), and the angular
equation fixes the full curve. This identifies every subsequential
limit. The closure on full classical histories in the
\href{newton-cusp-preparation-floor.md}{cusp construction} retains the
branch as state information.

\subsubsection{What a nonnegative spherical source
supplies}\label{what-a-nonnegative-spherical-source-supplies}

Newton's shell theorem gives a physically independent restriction on the
force law. Return to dimensional variables. Let \(G>0\) and let \(M(r)\)
be the enclosed mass of a finite nonnegative spherical source,
continuous at \(r>0\), with \(0<M(\infty)<\infty\). A point mass at zero
is allowed. The inward acceleration is \(g(r)=GM(r)/r^2\). For every
nonzero specific angular momentum \(j=|q\wedge\dot q|\), this force has
a unique global trajectory from each nonradial point state, including
when the radial speed vanishes. No derivative bound on the density is
needed.
\href{https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/diplomatic/NATP00082\#par469}{Newton,
1713, Book I, Propositions LXX--LXXI} (spherical shell theorems,
passages).

Write \(\Phi'=g\), \(\Phi(\infty)=0\) and \(U_j(r)=j^2/(2r^2)+\Phi(r)\)
for the energy per unit mass at a radial turn. Its unique stationary
radius \(R_c\) satisfies \(GR_c M(R_c)=j^2\): \(rM(r)\) increases
strictly wherever \(M(r)>0\). For every \(r>0\), monotonicity of the
enclosed mass gives \(\Phi(r)-\Phi(R_c)\ge GM(R_c)(1/R_c-1/r)\) on
either side of \(R_c\). Consequently

\[
 U_j(r)-U_j(R_c)\ \ge\ \frac{j^2}{2}
               \left(\frac1r-\frac1{R_c}\right)^2 . \tag{C5}
\]

A point source attains equality. Starting at the circular energy forces
\(r=R_c\). Elsewhere a radial turning point is simple, and its nonzero
radial acceleration fixes the continuation by the energy quadrature.
With nonzero radial velocity the same quadrature determines the motion
directly. Finally \(U_j(r)\ge j^2/(2r^2)-GM(\infty)/r\) keeps every
finite-energy orbit away from zero. The speed is bounded on that
exterior region, giving global continuation.

For (C1), \(r^2g(r)<1\) just outside the unit circle while \(g(1)=1\).
Its dimensionless Poisson density \((r^2g)'/(4\pi r^2)\) is signed. It
is constant in the core, zero beyond \(1+b\) and integrable to every
finite power in the shell. Indeed the sole unbounded factor there grows
as \(\log^4(1/|r-1|)\) and the integral for its \(p\)th power is bounded
by a constant times \(\int_{20}^{\infty} L^{4p}e^{-L}dL\). Continuity of
\(r^2g\) excludes a surface delta at the joins. The squared field
integral \(4\pi\int_0^\infty r^2g(r)^2dr\) is finite. Thus integrability
of the signed source and finiteness of its field integral allow the
order dependence in (C3).

This uniqueness survives source and time refinement together. Let
\(M_k\to M\) uniformly on compact radial intervals in \((0,\infty)\) for
continuous enclosed masses as above. Fix the initial nonradial state and
a finite time interval. Choose an annulus containing the whole limiting
orbit strictly in its interior. Cut off all forces beyond a slightly
larger annulus with the same cutoff, equal to one on the first. They now
converge uniformly to a continuous bounded field. The §3 compactness
argument, with the uniform force difference added to the residual,
applies to arbitrary positive time partitions and arbitrary choices of
impulse order. Every limit follows the unique orbit up to any first
exit, which the orbit never makes. The original polygons thus have the
same limit along every joint refinement. For a point source
\href{self-sufficiency-contrast.md}{the fixed-time Coulomb estimate,
§5a} also takes a supplied reduced action to zero at fixed
noncollisional initial position and momentum. Holding the nonzero
angular momentum of the orbit fixed is compatible with this limit.

\subsubsection{Uniform refinement over a set of trial
actions}\label{uniform-refinement-over-a-set-of-trial-actions}

Use (C1) again, in its dimensionless variables. The fixed terminal time
is \(T/\omega\) and an equal time cell lasts \(h/\omega\) in physical
units. The dimensional reference action is \(S_0=mR^2\omega\). Write
\(\epsilon=a/S_0\) for a trial reduced action \(a\). For \(\epsilon>0\)
take the canonical kinetic and potential operators on
\(L^2(\mathbb R^2)\), \(P=-i\epsilon\nabla\), and the normalized state

\[
 \psi_\epsilon(x)=(\pi\epsilon)^{-1/2}
 \exp[-|x-q_0|^2/(2\epsilon)+i v_0\cdot(x-q_0)/\epsilon].
\]

Its coordinate and momentum standard deviations per component in
physical units are \(R\sqrt{\epsilon/2}\) and
\(mR\omega\sqrt{\epsilon/2}\). Use the same state for either impulse
placement. Fix a smooth radial function \(0\le f\le1\), equal to zero
for \(r\le1-Y(T)/2\) and one for \(r\ge1+Y(T)/2\). Its transition width
in physical units is \(R Y(T)\), independent of the partition and
action. Thus changing \(a\) changes the preparation widths while the
spatial readout and its terminal time stay fixed. For a step \(s\) write
\(K_s=e^{-isU(Q)/\epsilon}\) and \(D_s=e^{-isP^2/(2\epsilon)}\). For
each positive partition \(\pi\) of \([0,T]\) and a choice \(\sigma\) of
the order in every cell, let \(p_{a,\pi,\sigma}\) be the final
expectation of \(f(Q)\). At \(a=0\) use the two classical factors (2) on
the point \((q_0,v_0)\) and evaluate \(f\).

For any nonempty set \(A\subset[0,\infty)\) of dimensional trial
actions, the following dichotomy holds for this one force, initial
family and \(f\):

\[
 \lim_{\delta\downarrow0}
 \sup_{\substack{a\in A,\ |\pi|\le\delta\\\sigma,\sigma'}}
 |p_{a,\pi,\sigma}-p_{a,\pi,\sigma'}|
 =\begin{cases}0,&\inf A>0,\\1,&\inf A=0.\end{cases} \tag{C6}
\]

\textbf{Arbitrarily small action.} At a fixed finite partition a
localized state follows its classical product as \(a\downarrow0\). For
the drift this follows from the exact shear \(Q\mapsto Q+sP\). A kick
shifts \(P\) by \(-s\nabla U(Q)\). For a state of unit norm localized
near \(q\) and any \(l>0\), the norm of its force fluctuation is bounded
by

\[
 \|[\nabla U(Q)-\nabla U(q)]\psi\|
 \le \omega_{\nabla U}(l)+2\|\nabla U\|_\infty
                                     \|(Q-q)\psi\|/l .
\]

Here \(\nabla U\) is bounded and uniformly continuous. Starting with the
Gaussian, induction propagates vanishing position and momentum
deviations for the finitely many factors, first sending \(a\) to zero
and then \(l\) to zero at each step. For all sufficiently fine uniform
meshes the classical terminal radii lie inside the opposite plateaus of
\(f\), by (C2)--(C4). If \(A\) accumulates at zero, take the
small-action limit at each such mesh within \(A\). If \(0\in A\) the
classical products already give the unit difference. This proves the
second line of (C6).

\textbf{Positive action floor.} For fixed \(\epsilon>0\) both products
have the same unitary continuum limit. The potential is a bounded real
multiplier.
\href{https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9939-1959-0108732-6}{Trotter, 1959}
(product formula). For the required uniformity, put
\(B_\epsilon(t)=D_{-t}U(Q)D_t/\epsilon\). In the interaction picture
each split product evolves with \(B_\epsilon\) frozen at the chosen
endpoint of each cell. On any compact positive \(\epsilon\) interval
these operators are uniformly bounded and jointly strongly continuous in
\(t,\epsilon\). The exact interaction-picture vector \(w_\epsilon(t)\)
is norm-continuous in both variables by its integral equation with
bounded generator and the norm-continuous initial Gaussian. Duhamel
bounds the product error by

\[
 T\sup_{|s-t|\le\delta}
        \|(B_\epsilon(s)-B_\epsilon(t))w_\epsilon(t)\|.
\]

This tends uniformly to zero on the compact parameter interval. For the
large-\(\epsilon\) tail, each split product differs from free evolution
by at most \(T\|U\|_\infty/\epsilon\) in operator norm, by telescoping.
These bounds prove the first line of (C6).

The order effect also has a definite scale on equal cells \(h=T/N\). The
products satisfy \((K_h D_h)^N=K_h(D_h K_h)^N K_h^{-1}\). Since \(f(Q)\)
commutes with the final kick, their score difference is bounded by the
pure-state trace distance between \(\psi_\epsilon\) and
\(K_h^{-1}\psi_\epsilon\). Removing the phase \(hU(q_0)/\epsilon\)
bounds this distance by \(3h/\sqrt\epsilon\) using (C1) and the initial
spatial rms width. More precisely, if \(h,\epsilon\to0\) with
\(h/\sqrt\epsilon\to\lambda<\infty\), write \(x=q_0+\sqrt\epsilon\,y\).
Differentiability at \(q_0\), where \(\nabla U(q_0)=(1,0)\), and
dominated convergence give overlap modulus \(e^{-\lambda^2/4}\) by the
Gaussian Fourier transform. Thus the limiting upper bound on the score
difference is \(\sqrt{1-e^{-\lambda^2/2}}\). Full separation requires
\(a/S_0=o(h^2)\) along a vanishing-action sequence. Conversely (C6)
allows some such sequences. For \(a/S_0=h\), the two constant orderings
agree in the limit in the sense that their difference vanishes.
\href{record-distance-path-length.md}{Pure-state distance} (standard
identity).

\subsubsection{The same refinement criterion for classical
ensembles}\label{the-same-refinement-criterion-for-classical-ensembles}

Could (C6) by itself establish that the width comes from a physical
quantum of action? Keep the force, the partitions, the two classical
maps, \(T\) and \(f\) fixed. On classical phase space take the density
\((\pi w)^{-2}\exp[-(|q-q_0|^2+|p-v_0|^2)/w]\) for \(w>0\) and the point
state for \(w=0\). Each position and momentum marginal matches the
initial quantum one at \(w=\epsilon\). Here \(w\) is freely supplied
classical dispersion. At every \(w\) the evolution uses only Newton's
equation.

For any nonempty width set \(B\subset[0,\infty)\) the exact dichotomy
(C6) holds for the classical scores with \(A\) replaced by \(B\). If
\(\inf B=0\), continuity of the finite products takes the concentrated
densities to their point-state answers, so (C3)--(C4) give the unit
difference. For \(w>0\), the initial hypersurfaces \(|q\wedge p|=1\)
have measure zero. Away from them every orbit is unique. The field is
locally Lipschitz away from \(r=1\). At \(r=1\), nonzero radial speed
gives unique passage by the energy integral and zero radial speed gives
a simple turn since \(r''=|q\wedge p|^2-1\ne0\). The bounded force then
gives convergence of arbitrary refining polygons for almost every
initial state. On \(w_0\le w\le w_1\) with \(w_0>0\) the densities have
a common integrable Gaussian envelope. Dominated convergence along any
sequence of partitions and placements proves uniform agreement there.

For the remaining tail, every terminal polygon position lies within
\(3T^2\) of the free position \(q+Tp\), by summing the bounded kicks.
The free position has density bounded by \(1/[\pi w(1+T^2)]\). Whenever
it lies outside the disk of radius \(1+Y(T)/2+3T^2\) about zero, every
ordering gives final score one. The probability of that disk tends to
zero as \(w\to\infty\), uniformly in the ordering.

There is a distinct finite-ratio bound for these ensembles. For the two
constant orderings the initial momentum shift is \(h\nabla U(q)\) up to
sign. At each \(q\) the equal-covariance Gaussian total-variation
formula gives \(\operatorname{erf}(h|\nabla U(q)|/(2\sqrt w))\).
Averaging over position bounds the score difference. This upper bound
tends to \(\operatorname{erf}(\lambda/2)\) at \(h/\sqrt w\to\lambda\),
\(w\downarrow0\). Both this and the quantum overlap bound concern
possible terminal differences. An actual difference between the two
dynamics at the critical scale requires their branch probabilities.
\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.08693\#page=3}{Devroye, Mehrabian and
Reddad, p.~3} (equal-covariance formula).

Newton's letter uses point-state uniqueness in the inverse-square conic
argument. Equation (C5) justifies it classically in a broader
gravitational class. Equation (C6) identifies what would exclude zero
for the specified canonical family. One must independently admit the
force (C1), require agreement of impulse placements uniformly over
allowed actions, and justify the chosen state space and dynamics. The
historical passage supplies none of these extensions.

\subsection{6. Consequence for STATE}\label{consequence-for-state}

Initial-value construction, continuation, endpoint caustics and
finite-time mergers have different hypotheses. Unbounded force slope
alone establishes no merger and no action floor. Uniform convergence of
the Lemma X coefficient holds even at a centre that admits delayed
departures. For outward radial forces (D2) decides uniqueness from one
moving orbit. The sign-changing counterexample (D4) delimits that
inference. The \href{newton-cusp-preparation-floor.md}{cusp preparation
theorem} uses an actual full-state merger and additional preparation
laws. The separate \href{balaban-newton-time-blocking.md}{endpoint
blocking argument} retains inverse Hessians, normalization and source
response; the \href{newton-nonlinear-time-cell.md}{nonlinear cell}
controls a supplied positive weight. Neither operation follows solely
from constructing a classical orbit. The moving-circle example now
distinguishes uniform agreement over trial actions from agreement at
each fixed positive action. A nonnegative spherical source admits
order-independent classical refinement, including at a circular orbit.
Classical ensembles of positive lower width also admit uniform
refinement. Which dynamics or composition law requires a physical action
in addition to a restriction on classical preparations remains open.
