\section{Balaban through Newton's polygons: a 0+1
lesson}\label{balaban-through-newtons-polygons-a-01-lesson}

\textbf{Lesson and written construction, 2026-10-27.} A useful way to
approach Balaban's method is to start with a familiar existence problem:
construct motion from finitely many force impulses, prove estimates that
survive arbitrary insertion of intermediate times, and take a limit.
Then ask what additional description must survive when an intermediate
position is integrated rather than solved for. The answer includes its
fluctuation law, normalization and generated interactions.

We work with one mechanical coordinate and time, keeping \(\hbar\)
explicit. The finite construction is a modern use of Newton's polygon
idea; it is not a proof about every historical edition or force polygon.
The Galileo inertial-line / falling-parabola comparison supplies our
action observable. No relativistic assumption or \(c\) is needed.

The ODE proof below is standard in method and claims no novelty. Its
purpose is to make the comparison concrete, including arbitrary unequal
time steps and either force-kick convention. The two retained-weight
calculations are in the
\href{balaban-newton-time-blocking.md}{time-blocking note} and the
\href{newton-nonlinear-time-cell.md}{nonlinear cell}. They specify their
proof status and source reading. Balaban's
\href{../docs/balaban/Balaban_UltravioletStability_ICM1986_1987.md}{ICM
overview}, printed pages 1260--1262 (text read), and
\href{../docs/balaban/Dimock_BalabanI_1108.1335v2.md}{Dimock I},
sections 2.1, 2.4, 4.5 and Theorem 14 (selected passages read), motivate
the comparison.

\subsection{1. Start with a finite Newton
polygon}\label{start-with-a-finite-newton-polygon}

Let \(m>0\) be the mass and \(f(q)=F(q)/m\) the acceleration. The
desired motion would satisfy

\[\dot q=v,\qquad\dot v=f(q),\qquad(q(0),v(0))=(q_0,v_0).\tag{1}\]

For now we do not assume that a solution exists. Given a positive time
step \(\tau\), define a drift and a force kick by

\[
 D_\tau(q,v)=(q+\tau v,v),\qquad
 K_\tau(q,v)=(q,v+\tau f(q)).
 \tag{2}
\]

A step can kick first, \(D_\tau K_\tau\), or drift first,
\(K_\tau D_\tau\). The first choice uses the force at the old position;
the second uses the force at the new position. Both give a finite
piecewise straight trajectory with impulses. Neither is assumed to agree
exactly with the unknown continuous motion.

Fix \(T>0\), take any finite positive partition of \([0,T]\), choose
either ordering in each cell, and compose the maps chronologically.
There is no equal-step or nesting requirement. When a time is inserted,
recompute the later states from the fixed initial state and the
prescribed force rule.

Existence of the limit requires two kinds of estimates. Stability bounds
how differences grow. Consistency bounds the error in the finite force
balance. The two together will construct the ODE solution.

\subsection{2. Lipschitz control supplies
stability}\label{lipschitz-control-supplies-stability}

Assume for this construction that

\[|f(q)-f(Q)|\le\Lambda|q-Q|\quad(q,Q\in\mathbb R),\qquad\Lambda\ge0.
\tag{3}\]

The quantity \(\Lambda\) has units of inverse time squared. Choose a
reference frequency \(\rho>0\) and use the length-valued phase-space
norm

\[
 \|(q,v)\|_\rho=|q|+\frac{|v|}{\rho},\qquad
 \alpha=\max(\rho,\Lambda/\rho),\quad
 \beta=\rho+\Lambda/\rho,\quad b=|f(0)|/\rho.
 \tag{4}
\]

There is no action constant in these definitions. Directly from (2),

\[
 \operatorname{Lip}(D_\tau)\le1+\rho\tau,\qquad
 \operatorname{Lip}(K_\tau)\le1+\Lambda\tau/\rho.
 \tag{5}
\]

Thus either cell map has Lipschitz constant at most \(e^{\beta\tau}\).
The constants multiply under composition, and their exponents add. Every
finite polygon therefore has the same upper amplification
\(e^{\beta T}\), independently of the number and placement of vertices.

We also need a bound on the states themselves, because consistency
involves the force along the polygon. For either cell map \(M_\tau\),

\[
 \|M_\tau z\|_\rho\le e^{\beta\tau}\|z\|_\rho
                         +\tau e^{\beta\tau}b.
 \tag{6}
\]

For a kick-first cell, the inhomogeneous term is actually at most
\(\tau(1+\rho\tau)b\); for a drift-first cell it is \(\tau b\).
Iterating (6) gives the common vertex bound

\[R=e^{\beta T}(\|z_0\|_\rho+Tb).\tag{7}\]

In particular \(|q_j|\le R\), \(|v_j|\le\rho R\), and
\(|f(q_j)|\le A:=|f(0)|+\Lambda R\). A very irregular partition has not
created a new force or stability constant.

\subsection{3. The polygons construct the ODE
solution}\label{the-polygons-construct-the-ode-solution}

Linearly interpolate both vertex coordinates to obtain
\(z_\pi(t)=(q_\pi(t),v_\pi(t))\). The displayed velocity is continuous;
it differs from the physical constant velocity along each straight
polygon segment by at most that cell's kick. This distinction disappears
with the mesh.

Let \(\delta=|\pi|\) be the maximum cell duration. In a kick-first cell,
with elapsed time \(s\in[0,\tau]\),

\[
 \dot q_\pi-v_\pi=(\tau-s)f(q_j),\qquad
 \dot v_\pi-f(q_\pi)=f(q_j)-f(q_\pi).
 \tag{8}
\]

In a drift-first cell the analogous expressions are \(-sf(q_{j+1})\) and
\(f(q_{j+1})-f(q_\pi)\). The bounds in (7) give, for almost every time,

\[
 \|\dot z_\pi-\mathcal F(z_\pi)\|_\rho\le C\delta,
 \quad\mathcal F(q,v)=(v,f(q)),\quad C=|f(0)|+2\Lambda R.
 \tag{9}
\]

The vector field \(\mathcal F\) is Lipschitz with constant \(\alpha\) in
the norm (4). Two polygons with the same initial state consequently
satisfy

\[
 \sup_{t\le T}\|z_\pi(t)-z_\sigma(t)\|_\rho
 \le C\frac{e^{\alpha T}-1}{\alpha}
                        (|\pi|+|\sigma|).
 \tag{10}
\]

To verify this estimate, integrate the two residual equations (9),
subtract, and apply the elementary integral Gronwall inequality. That
inequality follows by integrating the differential inequality for an
upper bound after multiplying by \(e^{-\alpha t}\).

Completeness of continuous path space now constructs a uniform limit
\(z(t)\). The residual integral tends to zero; the Lipschitz bound
passes \(\mathcal F(z_\pi)\) to \(\mathcal F(z)\) under the time
integral. Hence

\[z(t)=z_0+\int_0^t\mathcal F(z(s))\,ds.\tag{11}\]

The limit is differentiable and solves (1). Applying the same difference
estimate with zero residual proves uniqueness. Letting one mesh tend to
zero in (10) gives a quantitative error for any other partition. Both
kick orders, mixed arbitrarily, lead to the same solution. Since \(T\)
was arbitrary, the global Lipschitz hypothesis constructs forward motion
for every finite time.

This is the first analogy with Balaban: one proves bounds for each
finite description before appealing to a limiting object. The limit
follows from estimates uniform in the refinement. Nothing in this
argument requires choosing \(\hbar>0\).

\subsection{4. Local existence, continuation and endpoint caustics are
different}\label{local-existence-continuation-and-endpoint-caustics-are-different}

For a locally Lipschitz force one first works inside a bounded region. A
Lipschitz extension permits the construction above; a short-time bound
ensuring that the trajectory remains in the region makes the extension
irrelevant. Continuation requires additional control against escape from
all bounded phase-space regions.

For example, \(V(q)=\lambda q^4/4\), \(\lambda>0\), has unbounded force
derivative globally. A fixed energy bounds both \(q\) and \(p=mv\), so
its initial-value solution continues. The inverted quartic has a smooth,
locally Lipschitz force too, yet admits finite-time escape. The explicit
bounds and escape solution appear in the time-blocking note, section 6.
Continuity alone can give local existence; the Lipschitz assumption
above also supplies the uniqueness and quantitative stability used in
(10).

An endpoint problem fixes \(q(0)\) and \(q(T)\) rather than initial
velocity. It has a separate possible singularity. For the harmonic
oscillator the full phase-space derivative is

\[
 \begin{pmatrix}
 \cos\omega T&\sin(\omega T)/\omega\\
 -\omega\sin(\omega T)&\cos\omega T
 \end{pmatrix},\qquad \det=1.
 \tag{12}
\]

The position response to initial velocity is \(\sin(\omega T)/\omega\).
It vanishes at a conjugate time while the full phase-space map stays
invertible. The corresponding endpoint action Hessian can fail to invert
even though the initial-value motion remains unique. The time-blocking
note gives a finite-polygon determinant that approaches this singularity
under refinement.

Therefore a failure of one position-only generating function is not, by
itself, a failure of Newton's dynamics. A physical completion argument
has to establish which extra information the complete theory needs and
why it cannot be retained classically.

\subsection{5. Retain an intermediate time as a fluctuating
variable}\label{retain-an-intermediate-time-as-a-fluctuating-variable}

The ODE polygon solves for its intermediate states. A different
construction assigns weights and integrates an unobserved intermediate
position. For the positive free kernel,

\[
 k_{T,\hbar}(y,x)=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar T}}
       \exp\left[-\frac{m(y-x)^2}{2\hbar T}\right],\qquad\hbar>0,
 \tag{13}
\]

splitting \(T=u+v\) gives a conditional inserted position with

\[
 \mu=\frac{vx+uy}{T},\qquad
 \operatorname{Var}(Z\mid x,y)=\frac{\hbar uv}{mT}.
 \tag{14}
\]

A constant-force Newton parabola shifts this vertex from its chord by
\(-Fuv/(2m)\). The associated hat deformation has action
\(F^2uvT/(8m)\). For equal-noise Gaussian bridge laws the relative
entropy between the shifted and unshifted vertex laws is that action
divided by \(\hbar\). The precise normalization and physical premises
are recorded in the time-blocking note, equations (15a)--(15b).

The positive Gaussian kernel is a Euclidean construction. The real-time
weight uses \(e^{iS^R/\hbar}\) and Fresnel integration; its quadratic
second moment is not a probability covariance. Changing between them
requires more than replacing a symbol in a fluctuation estimate.

Here the role of the action constant is visible: it sets the fluctuation
width and the strength of action differences in the weight. Ordinary ODE
convergence controls the mean path construction; it does not fix that
extra law.

\subsection{6. What a nonlinear time block actually
produces}\label{what-a-nonlinear-time-block-actually-produces}

For a symmetric two-cell Euclidean action, put \(a=mT/(uv)\) and
\(w=T/2\). Retain

\[
 A_\hbar(\mu)=-\hbar\log\left[
 \sqrt{\frac{a}{2\pi\hbar}}\int
 e^{-[a(z-\mu)^2/2+wV(z)]/\hbar}dz\right].
 \tag{15}
\]

This whole function accompanies the coarse kinetic term and the endpoint
potential terms. Its derivatives have a useful interpretation:

\[
 A_\hbar'=a(\mu-\mathbb EZ),\qquad
 A_\hbar''=a-\frac{a^2}{\hbar}\operatorname{Var}(Z).
 \tag{16}
\]

Thus an eliminated fluctuation changes the retained response. For convex
\(V\), the nonlinear-cell note proves \(0\le A_\hbar''\le a\) and an
explicit normalized tail bound about the conditional minimizing
position. It also proves a simultaneous bound for large fluctuations at
nonadjacent sites in one halving. No bad region is dropped from the
integral.

Classical minimization gives the envelope
\(A_0(\mu)=\min_z[a(z-\mu)^2/2+wV(z)]\). It too has curvature between
zero and \(a\). Coarse regularity alone therefore does not distinguish
the two operations.

The quartic centre does distinguish them quantitatively. For
\(V(z)=\lambda z^4/4\), \(\lambda>0\), \(A_0''(0)=0\) while

\[
 A_\hbar''(0)>0\quad(\hbar>0),\qquad
 A_\hbar''(0)\sim\frac{3w\lambda\hbar}{a}
        \quad\left(\frac{w\lambda\hbar}{a^2}\downarrow0\right).
 \tag{17}
\]

The nonlinear-cell note gives finite inequalities, not just the
asymptotic formula, and inverts them to a model-conditional action
interval. This is an example of a generated interaction: a purely
quartic bare potential acquires quadratic curvature after fluctuation
integration. The action multiplier remains supplied in (15).

Balaban organizes successive effective densities so that such
information can be estimated at the next scale. The analogous task for
ODE existence is to preserve enough stability and consistency
information to pass from all finite polygons to one motion. The objects
of the two existence problems differ: an ODE solution is a trajectory; a
weighted continuum construction includes a law or propagator over
trajectories.

The one-dimensional chain makes an elementary first step possible:
conditioned on retained times, distinct missing intervals are
independent. Their interactions can nevertheless change functional form
at each elimination. Closure of a finite list of polynomial coefficients
is an additional assertion that must be checked.

\subsection{8. Consequence for STATE}\label{consequence-for-state}

The requested \(0+1\)/ODE lesson is developed together with a causal
construction of nonlinear initial-value motion on arbitrary unequal
partitions, allowing mixed kick orders. It complements the endpoint
Green-function calculation and explains the normalized nonlinear cell.
The research question is now sharper: which independent physical law
selects the weight and its action multiplier, beyond the regularity
already available from classical motion? The next candidate test is
branch competition in a nonlinear retained interaction, keeping the full
initial-value description as its classical control. These are written
derivations and a pedagogical comparison, not an independent proof of
action necessity.
