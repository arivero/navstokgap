\section{Time blocking, Newton polygons and explicit quantum
action}\label{time-blocking-newton-polygons-and-explicit-quantum-action}

A bounded inward central force admits a unique classical passage through
the centre at nonzero speed. At a zero-speed encounter with a bounded
repulsive force, two continuations of the same incoming orbit obey the
same force balance after every time insertion and have equal action on
every cell. A detector at a fixed time distinguishes them. Two shrinking
source profiles select the opposite returns, yet the fraction of
incoming classical flux that reaches either core tends to zero as an
exact power of its radius.
\hyperref[central-force-passage-under-point-insertion]{Central-force
passage}.

For repulsive inverse-square scattering, three incident energies make a
different test possible. A sum of measured angular yields must vanish
for every common classical distribution of impact parameters, including
a sharply concentrated beam. An observed excess over the calibration
error gives a lower bound on a trial action in the spin-zero Coulomb
exchange model.
\hyperref[a-three-energy-test-of-central-force-trajectories]{Three-energy
test}.

Electron transmission through argon supplies a single-energy comparison
without identical-particle exchange. A finite annulus of exterior Newton
orbits would miss the measured exit aperture if the polarization force
has at least half its asymptotic strength beyond \(0.50\) nm, with the
upper control specified below. The published attenuation fixes the size
of the discrepancy such a force calibration would have to explain. For a
freely moving charge coupled to a localized, linearly polarizable
target, two clocked response measurements suffice to bound how much the
delayed force changes the outgoing angle.
\hyperref[an-exterior-force-test-with-argon]{Argon transmission} and
\hyperref[two-pulse-tests-for-a-freely-moving-charge]{free-charge
calibration}.

At lower energy the relevant orbits move out into the weak field of the
atom. Their angular momenta tend to zero, yet they give a positive lower
limit for relative speed times momentum-transfer cross section. Three
finite work readings give a separate test at a specified energy,
allowing charge penetration and nonlinear electrostatic relaxation.
\hyperref[three-work-readings-and-the-low-energy-limit]{Work
measurements and the threshold rate}.

\subsection{1. Actions and the constant to be
investigated}\label{actions-and-the-constant-to-be-investigated}

Let \(m,T>0\), \(x,y\in\mathbb R\), and \(\pi=(0=t_0<\cdots<t_N=T)\).
Write \(\tau_j=t_{j+1}-t_j\) and \(w_j=(\tau_{j-1}+\tau_j)/2\) for
interior vertices. For a potential \(V\) define

\[
 S_\pi^\sigma(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}
 \frac{m(q_{j+1}-q_j)^2}{2\tau_j}
 +\sigma\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\frac{\tau_j}{2}
       [V(q_j)+V(q_{j+1})],\qquad q_0=x,\ q_N=y.
 \tag{1}
\]

The real-time Newton action is \(S_\pi^R=S_\pi^{-1}\), with weight
\(e^{iS_\pi^R/\hbar}\) when \(\hbar>0\). The Euclidean action is
\(S_\pi^E=S_\pi^{+1}\), with weight \(e^{-S_\pi^E/\hbar}\). Their
stationary equations have opposite potential signs. They must not be
interchanged when interpreting a background as a Newton trajectory. At
\(\hbar=0\) these exponential expressions are not defined; the classical
stationary equation is defined directly. No relativistic premise or
\(c\) is used in this calculation. Ordinary Planck action is
\(h_P=2\pi\hbar\).

For real time put \(f=-V'/m\), the acceleration. Section 2 needs only a
Lipschitz function \(f\), even without first specifying \(V\). A
Lipschitz condition is a sufficient uniqueness and stability condition;
continuity alone can give local existence. Neither statement alone
guarantees continuation for all times or uniqueness of a two-endpoint
problem.

\subsection{2. Arbitrary intermediate
times}\label{arbitrary-intermediate-times}

Let \(\ell(t)=x+(y-x)t/T\) and

\[
 G(t,s)=\min(t,s)-\frac{ts}{T}.
 \tag{2}
\]

This is the positive Dirichlet Green function of \(-d^2/dt^2\). Define
the polygon map, with piecewise-linear output, by

\[
 (\mathcal T_\pi q)(t)=\ell(t)
       -\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}w_jG(t,t_j)f(q(t_j)).
 \tag{3}
\]

\textbf{Lemma 1 (the exact row sum).} At every vertex,

\[
 \sum_{j=1}^{N-1}w_jG(t_i,t_j)=\frac{t_i(T-t_i)}2.
 \tag{4}
\]

At every \(t\in[0,T]\) the sum is at most \(T^2/8\).

\emph{Proof.} For fixed \(t_i\), the function \(s\mapsto G(t_i,s)\) is
linear on each partition interval and vanishes at the endpoints. Its
trapezoidal integral is therefore exact, and its integral is
\(t_i(T-t_i)/2\). As a function of \(t\), the sum in (4) is the linear
interpolant of that concave quadratic. It lies below the quadratic,
whose maximum is \(T^2/8\). This proof uses no ratio between consecutive
time steps. \(\square\)

Assume \(|f(u)-f(v)|\le\Lambda|u-v|\) for all \(u,v\), and put

\[\theta=\frac{\Lambda T^2}{8}<1.\tag{5}\]

\textbf{Proposition 2 (one bound for every polygon).} The map (3) has a
unique fixed polygon. Its slopes \(v_j=(q_{j+1}-q_j)/\tau_j\) satisfy

\[v_j-v_{j-1}=w_jf(q_j).\tag{6}\]

The continuous map

\[
 (\mathcal Tq)(t)=\ell(t)-\int_0^T G(t,s)f(q(s))\,ds
 \tag{7}
\]

has a unique fixed point, which is \(C^2\) and solves \(\ddot q=f(q)\)
with the two prescribed endpoints. Every sequence of partitions with
\(|\pi|\to0\) converges to this solution. Neither nesting nor equal
intervals is required.

\emph{Proof.} Lemma 1 bounds the Lipschitz constants of (3) and (7) in
the supremum norm by \(\theta\). Repeated application converges because
the successive differences form a geometric series. This proves
existence and uniqueness in the complete polygon space or in
\(C([0,T])\). The derivative of \(G(\cdot,t_j)\) jumps by \(-1\) at
\(t_j\), giving (6). Differentiating (7) gives the continuous equation.

Inserting a vertex changes its neighbours' weights \(w_j\) as well as
the new solution. It does not add one kick while freezing the old force
history. Retained vertices need not remain fixed when the trapezoidal
action itself is refined.

For a quantitative limit, write \(B=\|f(q)\|_\infty\) and
\(U=\|\dot q\|_\infty\) for the continuous solution. For every fixed
\(t\), \(s\mapsto G(t,s)f(q(s))\) is Lipschitz with constant at most
\(B+T\Lambda U/4\). On an interval of length \(\tau\), replacing its
integral by the endpoint trapezoid has error at most half that constant
times \(\tau^2\). Hence

\[
 \|q_\pi-q\|_\infty\le
 \frac{T|\pi|}{2(1-\theta)}
       \left(B+\frac{T\Lambda U}{4}\right).
 \tag{8}
\]

Indeed, subtract the two fixed-point equations and absorb the
\(\theta\|q_\pi-q\|_\infty\) term. The constants in (8) belong to the
fixed solution and interval, not to the partition. \(\square\)

For a force Lipschitz only in a tube \(|q(t)-\ell(t)|\le r\), the same
proof holds within that tube if \(\theta<1\) and

\[
 \frac{T^2}{8}\big(B_0+\Lambda r\big)\le r,
 \qquad B_0=\sup_t|f(\ell(t))|.
 \tag{9}
\]

This makes the closed tube invariant. Uniqueness is then asserted only
inside it. Equation (9) is a finite version of a small-field domain that
survives the construction; it cannot justify discarding paths outside it
from a statistical or oscillatory integral.

\subsection{3. The action Hessian and
elimination}\label{the-action-hessian-and-elimination}

Let \(\mathsf K_\pi\) be the Dirichlet stiffness matrix, with diagonal
\(1/\tau_{j-1}+1/\tau_j\) and adjacent entries \(-1/\tau_j\), and let
\(\mathsf W_\pi=\operatorname{diag}(w_j)\). The tent function (2) gives

\[
 (\mathsf K_\pi^{-1})_{ij}=G(t_i,t_j),\qquad
 \|\mathsf K_\pi^{-1}\mathsf W_\pi\|_{\infty\to\infty}
       \le\frac{T^2}{8}.
 \tag{10}
\]

The first identity follows by taking the slope jump of that tent at each
node; the second is Lemma 1. In particular, for \(V\in C^2\) with
\(|V''|\le m\Lambda\), the Hessian of (1) obeys

\[
 \mathsf H_\pi^\sigma
   =m\mathsf K_\pi+\sigma\mathsf W_\pi
             \operatorname{diag}(V''(q_j))
   \ \ge\ m(1-\theta)\mathsf K_\pi.
 \tag{11}
\]

To see the quadratic-form inequality, the nonnegative matrix
\(\mathsf K_\pi^{-1}\mathsf W_\pi\) has spectral radius at most its
maximum row sum. It is similar to the positive symmetric matrix
\(\mathsf W_\pi^{1/2}\mathsf K_\pi^{-1}\mathsf W_\pi^{1/2}\). Thus
\(\mathsf W_\pi\le(T^2/8)\mathsf K_\pi\), which proves (11). The factor
\(1-\theta\) is uniform over arbitrary partitions.

For a physical force source \(J_j\), inserted as a linear action term
\(\sum_jw_jJ_jq_j\), the stationary response therefore has the bound

\[
 \|\delta q\|_\infty\le
 \frac{T^2}{8m(1-\theta)}\|\delta J\|_\infty.
 \tag{12}
\]

Here the bound is for the differential response. It follows directly by
factoring \(\mathsf H_\pi^\sigma\) as
\(m\mathsf K_\pi(I+\mathsf K_\pi^{-1}\mathsf W_\pi
\sigma\operatorname{diag}(V'')/m)\) and using a Neumann series.

With the global curvature bound, (11) makes the finite action coercive
and strictly convex in its interior vertices. Eliminating some vertices
by minimization is then unambiguous, and its Hessian is the Schur
complement

\[
 \mathsf H_{BB}-\mathsf H_{BA}\mathsf H_{AA}^{-1}\mathsf H_{AB}.
 \tag{13}
\]

Successive eliminations associate. With only a local tube, this is a
statement on the chosen nondegenerate branch. In particular, for the
real-time positive quartic potential the unrestricted action is
unbounded below, as the Rivero note already records; local control is
not a global minimization theorem. Nor does the eliminated action
generally retain only the original kinetic and potential terms.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{4. What the same inverse says when \(\hbar\)
is
present}{4. What the same inverse says when \textbackslash hbar is present}}\label{what-the-same-inverse-says-when-hbar-is-present}

At a Euclidean background, its Gaussian fluctuation reference has
density proportional to \(\exp[-\eta^T\mathsf H_\pi^E\eta/(2\hbar)]\)
and covariance

\[
 \mathsf C_{\pi,\hbar}^E=\hbar(\mathsf H_\pi^E)^{-1},\qquad
 (\mathsf C_{\pi,\hbar}^E)_{jj}
 \le\frac{\hbar}{m(1-\theta)}\frac{t_j(T-t_j)}{T}
 \le\frac{\hbar T}{4m(1-\theta)}.
 \tag{14}
\]

These are exact Gaussian statements. For a nonlinear action they do not
assert the covariance bound for its full measure without a further
argument. In real time, normalized quadratic Fresnel integration gives
the algebraic centered second moment \(i\hbar(\mathsf H_\pi^R)^{-1}\),
which is not a probability covariance.

For one block of Gaussian variables, integration retains both the Schur
complement (13) and a determinant factor: respectively
\((2\pi\hbar)^{|A|/2}(\det\mathsf H_{AA}^E)^{-1/2}\) in Euclidean time
and \((2\pi i\hbar)^{|A|/2}(\det\mathsf H_{AA}^R)^{-1/2}\) when the real
Hessian is positive. Determinants, normalization and any higher
interactions must accompany the stationary action. These are the extra
data that Balaban's effective-density viewpoint instructs us to retain.

Equations (11)--(14) identify the connection to ODE control: the
curvature constant that makes the background construction stable also
controls the Gaussian fluctuations for every positive supplied
\(\hbar\). They do not create the multiplier \(\hbar\) in (14).

\subsection{5. Written controls: Galileo and the
oscillator}\label{written-controls-galileo-and-the-oscillator}

\textbf{Constant force.} For \(V(q)=-Fq\), \(\Lambda=0\). Equation (7)
is exactly

\[q(t)=\ell(t)+\frac{F}{2m}t(t-T).\tag{15}\]

By (4), every polygon has these same vertex positions. The fluctuation
Hessian is the free one for either action sign, and the Euclidean
covariance is \((\hbar/m)G(t_i,t_j)\). For a single inserted midpoint of
a cell of length \(T\), its variance is \(\hbar T/(4m)\); for an unequal
cut at \(u\) it is \(\hbar u(T-u)/(mT)\). The deterministic vertex
survives at \(\hbar=0\); the Gaussian width tends to zero. This uses
precisely the Galileo comparison already normalized in the refinement
note.

\textbf{One arbitrary cut (Terra's connection).} Split \(T=u+v\) and let
\(H_u\) be the piecewise-linear hat of height one at \(u\), zero at the
cell endpoints. Then

\[
 \int_0^T(H_u')^2dt=\frac{T}{uv},\qquad
 a_F=-\frac{Fuv}{2m},\qquad
 \frac m2\int_0^T(a_FH_u')^2dt
       =\frac{F^2uvT}{8m}=K_T-K_u-K_v.
 \tag{15a}
\]

where \(K_s=F^2s^3/(24m)\). The free bridge's conditional
inserted-vertex innovation has variance \(s_u^2=\hbar uv/(mT)\). For the
two equal-variance Gaussian vertex laws, with means separated by
\(a_F\), direct integration of their log-density ratio gives

\[
 D\big(N(a_F,s_u^2)\,\|\,N(0,s_u^2)\big)
       =\frac{a_F^2}{2s_u^2}
       =\frac{K_T-K_u-K_v}{\hbar}.
 \tag{15b}
\]

Here \(D\) is relative entropy in nats. Successive bridge innovations
factor into independent Gaussian hat coefficients: conditioned on a
cell's endpoints, its inserted value has the variance above, and the two
residual bridges are independent. The hats are also orthogonal in the
Dirichlet energy because each finer hat has zero derivative integral
inside a cell where an older hat has constant derivative.

Thus classical vertex elimination, conditional Gaussian normalization
and the cut energy are related by one Schur factorization. The
cut-measure note already proves the additive action budget; the useful
connection here is to the blocking variables. Equation (15b) assumes
these Gaussian laws. It does not establish a physical bound on the
information in complete apparatus records.

\textbf{Oscillator and caustics.} Let \(V=m\omega^2q^2/2\),
\(\omega>0\). Its initial-value equation has the unique explicit
solution \(q(t)=q(0)\cos\omega t+\dot q(0)\sin\omega t/\omega\) for all
times. Nevertheless its two-endpoint problem loses uniqueness at
\(T=\pi/\omega\) when \(y=-x\); for other \(y\) it has no solution at
that time. A singular boundary Hessian therefore need not mean a failure
of initial-value mechanics.

On a uniform \(N\)-cell partition, \(\tau=T/N\), \(N\ge2\), the first
Dirichlet eigenvalue of the difference operator is
\(4\tau^{-2}\sin^2(\pi/(2N))\). Its Hessian first becomes singular at

\[
 T_N=\frac{2N}{\omega}\sin\frac{\pi}{2N}
       \longrightarrow\frac\pi\omega.
 \tag{16}
\]

This checks the partition-independent sufficient window
\(\omega^2T^2<8\): \(T_2=\sqrt8/\omega\), while the continuum caustic is
later. An ODE Lipschitz constant cannot exclude those later caustics.

For an exact Gaussian control, suppose \(T<\sqrt8/\omega\) and set
\(\alpha=2\arcsin(\omega\tau/2)\). The determinant ratio is

\[
 R_N=\frac{\det\mathsf H_\pi^R}{\det(m\mathsf K_\pi)}
     =\frac{\sin(N\alpha)}{N\sin\alpha}
       \longrightarrow\frac{\sin(\omega T)}{\omega T}.
 \tag{17}
\]

Indeed the determinant of the tridiagonal matrix with diagonal
\(2-\omega^2\tau^2=2\cos\alpha\) and off-diagonal \(-1\) satisfies
\(D_n=2\cos\alpha D_{n-1}-D_{n-2}\), with \(D_0=1,D_1=2\cos\alpha\).
Thus \(D_{N-1}=\sin(N\alpha)/\sin\alpha\); the free determinant is
\(N\). For \(N=2\), (17) gives \(R_2=1-\omega^2\tau^2/2\), also obtained
by completing the single midpoint square.

With the usual free normalization for each cell, finite Gaussian
integration gives the real-time kernel

\[
 K^\pi_\hbar(y,x)=
 \sqrt{\frac{m}{2\pi i\hbar T}}\,R_N^{-1/2}
           e^{iS_\pi^R(q_\pi)/\hbar}.
 \tag{18}
\]

The prefactor follows from \(\det\mathsf K_\pi=T/\prod_j\tau_j\) and
completing the quadratic form. The determinant identity follows by the
same tridiagonal recurrence, or by successive free one-vertex
eliminations. Equation (18) retains the same \(\hbar\) at every depth.
Reversing the sign of the oscillator term would put
\(1+\omega^2\tau^2/2\) in the two-cell determinant and remove the
real-time conjugate point. This sign test distinguishes the Euclidean
and Newton constructions.

\subsection{6. Large fields, continuation and the next scale
question}\label{large-fields-continuation-and-the-next-scale-question}

A local Lipschitz bound controls a local construction. To continue an
initial-value solution, one also needs control preventing escape from
every bounded phase-space region. In the confining example
\(V(q)=\lambda q^4/4\), \(\lambda>0\), conservation of a fixed classical
energy \(E>0\) gives

\[
 |q|\le R_E=(4E/\lambda)^{1/4},\qquad
 |p|\le\sqrt{2mE},\qquad
 |f'|\le\frac{3\lambda R_E^2}{m}.
 \tag{19}
\]

These bounds permit continuation of the initial-value solution. Applying
the local polygon lemma to a whole tube may require a larger radius than
\(R_E\) and the invariance condition (9). For the inverted quartic,
\(q(t)=q_0/[1-q_0\sqrt{\lambda/(2m)}t]\), \(q_0>0\), is instead a
finite-time escape solution. Its force is locally Lipschitz everywhere.
This is an explicit reason to distinguish local control from
continuation. Balaban's large-field estimates address weighted integrals
over all field regions; that is a statistical obligation distinct from
trajectory continuation.

Keeping \(\hbar\) explicit in the positive quartic Euclidean action, set
\(t=Ts\) and \(q=\sqrt{\hbar T/m}\,z\). Then

\[
 \frac{S^E}{\hbar}
 =\int_0^1\left[\frac{(z')^2}{2}+\frac g4z^4\right]ds,
 \qquad g=\frac{\lambda\hbar T^3}{m^2}.
 \tag{20}
\]

For the zero-endpoint free reference, the Brownian-bridge reflection
bound is

\[
 \mathbb P\!\left(\sup|q|>b\sqrt{\hbar T/m}\right)
       \le2e^{-2b^2},\qquad b>0.
 \tag{21}
\]

It follows by applying the positive and negative one-sided reflection
bounds and a union bound; the Rivero note, section 3, supplies the same
calculation. On this small-field tube the real quartic curvature gives
\(\theta\le3b^2g/8\). Thus the natural perturbation parameter combines
force curvature, the observation interval and the explicitly supplied
quantum width. For the interacting normalized measure, its denominator
and its own large-field contribution still require control; (21) alone
is not that result.

The next useful test is to retain the normalized fluctuation and
large-field terms through repeated time insertion, and determine which
physical premise would fix their action multiplier independently of the
weight \(e^{-S^E/\hbar}\) or \(e^{iS^R/\hbar}\). The existing
\href{rivero-1998-conjecture-central-forces.md}{quartic zero-limit
theorem} prevents treating refinement consistency alone as that premise.
A loss of boundary invertibility also needs the oscillator countertest
above before it is used to argue for completion of Newton's dynamics.

A complementary construction suggested by Terra is to control the
initial-value polygon flow and its tangent map under arbitrary
insertion. Their composition is causal and remains meaningful through
the oscillator's boundary caustics. Comparing that description with the
endpoint action would separate loss of one coordinate description from
an actual need for additional physical dynamics.

\subsection{Central-force passage under point
insertion}\label{central-force-passage-under-point-insertion}

In the 1713 \emph{Principia}, Book I, Proposition I, Corollary 4
compares forces by the inward sagittas of arcs traversed in equal times
as the arcs shrink. In Proposition VI Newton explicitly points to the
new Corollary 4 of Lemma X as another proof of the orbital force rule.
Does this local force information determine where the body emerges from
a small neighbourhood of the centre? Use one spatial position at a fixed
time as the readout.
\href{https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/diplomatic/NATP00077}{1687,
Book I, Lemma X and Proposition I} and
\href{https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/diplomatic/NATP00082}{1713,
Book I, Lemma X and Propositions I and VI} (diplomatic transcriptions,
passages).

\textbf{Finite inward force.} Take \(m>0\) and \(F(q)=-f(r)q/r\),
\(r=|q|\), with \(f\ge0\), continuous force at zero, locally Lipschitz
away from zero and \(|F|\le F_*\). The potential \(V\) has its minimum
at the centre. Nonzero angular momentum prevents arrival there at finite
energy. A radial arrival from a point with \(E>V(0)\) crosses with the
nonzero velocity fixed by its incoming direction and energy. The radial
travel integral \(\int dr/\sqrt{2(E-V(r))/m}\) fixes its clock on either
side. At the minimum energy a body at rest at the centre stays there by
energy conservation. These statements give uniqueness through the centre
without bounding \(f'\). With a globally bounded force, compactness and
uniqueness then give convergence of the usual kick--drift polygons on
each finite time interval, including for changing positive time
partitions. \href{balaban-ode-lesson.md}{Classical polygon construction
and compactness argument} (passage).

The impulse estimate is uniform over these force profiles. Suppose a ray
enters a ball of radius \(b\) at speed at least \(v_0>0\) and
\(bF_*/m\le v_0^2/2\). Attraction gives \(|\dot q|\ge v_0\) inside.
Hence \((r^2)''=2|\dot q|^2+2q\cdot F/m\ge v_0^2\). Integrating on each
side of closest approach bounds the time inside by \(2\sqrt2\,b/v_0\)
and the change in momentum by \(2\sqrt2\,F_*b/v_0\). In particular no
shrinking ball with this fixed force bound can reverse a finite incoming
momentum. This is a classical restriction on trajectories.

\textbf{A bounded central force at a turning point.} Fix a ruler
distance \(R\), inertial mass \(m\) and \(A>0\) with units
energy/length\(^{3/2}\). In three spatial dimensions take
\(V_0(r)=-Ar^{3/2}\) up to \(2R\). The force points outward and tends
continuously to zero at the origin. For \(r>2R\) continue its magnitude
by the inverse square law matched at \(2R\). For each \(0<b<R\), keep
this exterior potential for \(r\ge b\) and use
\(V_{b,\pm}(r)=-A b^{3/2}v_\pm(r/b)\) inside, with

\[
 v_+(u)=\tfrac14+\tfrac34u^2,\qquad
 v_-(u)=1-\tfrac32(1-u)+\tfrac38(1-u)^4,\qquad 0\le u\le1.
\]

Both profiles have \(v(1)=1\) and \(v'(1)=3/2\). Both derivatives
increase from zero, with \(v_-'(u)=\tfrac32[1-(1-u)^3]\). Thus every
core has a finite, outward, radially nondecreasing force and a Lipschitz
gradient of the potential. A spherical distribution of like charges can
source each profile because its enclosed charge is proportional to the
nondecreasing function \(r^2|F(r)|\). The maximum force difference is at
most \(3A\sqrt b/2\) and the maximum potential difference is
\(3A b^{3/2}/8\). Each force converges uniformly to \(F_0\), since all
three magnitudes inside \(b\) are at most \(3A\sqrt b/2\) and they agree
outside. At the centre \(v_+(0)=1/4\) and \(v_-(0)=-1/8\). A diameter
orbit of energy zero passes the first centre and turns before the
second. Here zero energy is referred to \(V_0(0)\) in both runs. The
incoming state at \(q=R e_1\) has the same inward momentum
\(p_R=\sqrt{2mA}R^{3/4}\) for every \(b\).

Write \(p_b=\sqrt{2mA}b^{3/4}\), \(t_b=mb/p_b\) and \(J_b=bp_b\). Let
\(u_+=0\) and let \(u_-\) be the simple zero of \(v_-\). The time and
ordinary mechanical action between entering the core and leaving it are
respectively

\[
 2t_b\int_{u_\pm}^1\frac{du}{\sqrt{v_\pm(u)}},
 \qquad 2J_b\int_{u_\pm}^1\sqrt{v_\pm(u)}\,du .
\]

At \(E=0\), \(\int p\cdot dq\) equals the time action. All four
integrals are finite. Inserting intermediate points on the trajectory
adds the corresponding integrals exactly. Halving \(b\) scales the time
by \(2^{-1/4}\) and the action by \(2^{-7/4}\) in either core. The
exterior inward travel time to \(b\) is \(4(t_R-t_b)\), where
\(t_R=mR/p_R\). Thus at the \emph{same clock time} \(T=8t_R\) the two
positions tend to \(-Re_1\) and \(+Re_1\). The trajectories are unique
and reversible for each fixed \(b\).

\textbf{Point insertion with the source held fixed.} Now use the single
potential \(V_0\) throughout. Put \(t_*=4t_R\), so \(T=2t_*\). Through
the common arrival the signed diameter coordinate is
\(x(t)=R(1-t/t_*)^4\). After arrival the two continuations are
\(x_\pm(t)=\pm R(t/t_*-1)^4\), with \(p=m\dot x\). Both have zero
velocity and acceleration at the join. From rest there, the signed force
estimate \(2m x_\pm(t_*+\delta)/\delta^2\) tends to zero on both
branches. Newton's short-time measurement returns the correct force even
where it cannot determine the later side. For any outgoing time cell
their actions coincide since \(m\dot x^2/2-V_0(x)\) is the same for the
two signs. On the first duration \(\delta\) after arrival it is the
integral of \(m\dot x^2\), namely \(16mR^2\delta^7/(7t_*^8)\). The
finite-force cusp, its waiting solutions and reversible classical
dynamics on entire histories are established in the
\href{newton-cusp-preparation-floor.md}{arrival argument} (passage).

One can test this on time nodes using only the given force law. On a
cell of duration \(\tau\), interpolate \(F_0\) at its initial, midpoint
and final positions by a quadratic \(B(u)\) in elapsed time. At
\(s=\tau/2,\tau\) impose the impulse and displacement balances

\[
 p(s)=p(0)+\int_0^s B(u)\,du,\qquad
 x(s)=x(0)+s p(0)/m+\frac1m\int_0^s(s-u)B(u)\,du .
\]

Start with a partition containing \(t_*\). On each branch the force in
time is quadratic on each side of the join, so its interpolant is exact.
Both sets of nodes solve the \emph{same} implicit cell equations on
every subdivision, however fine. Selecting a root would supply a branch
rule. Grant a detector independent position and clock error bounds
\(\delta_x\le R/4\) and \(\delta_t\le t_R/4\), held fixed as the mesh
and \(b\) shrink. Throughout that clock window the limiting distance is
at least \(R(15/16)^4>R/2\). The explicit exterior travel time gives
uniform convergence there for the two finite-core returns as well.
Recording the side therefore separates them at these fixed resolutions.
\href{https://dlmf.nist.gov/3.3.i}{Lagrange interpolation, DLMF §3.3(i)}
(passage).

\textbf{What the incoming flux sees.} At the incoming sphere \(r=R\) on
the same energy shell, write \(p_\perp\) for the two tangential momentum
components and \(L=R|p_\perp|\). The normalized inward Liouville flux is
uniform on \(|p_\perp|<p_R\) for each entry point: the normal speed
cancels the Jacobian of the energy delta function. A ray reaches \(b\)
precisely when \(L<J_b\), up to tangency. Outside that disk both motions
agree at every time. Consequently even the joint incoming and outgoing
record has total-variation distance bounded by

\[
  (J_b/J_R)^2=(b/R)^{7/2},\qquad J_R=Rp_R .
\]

A preparation of density at most \(C\) relative to that flux has the
same bound times \(C\), by coupling the same incoming rays. This
normalizes crossings of a section at fixed energy. The diameter
preparation is singular with respect to flux. At \(L=0\), an energy
error smaller than \(Ab^{3/2}/8\) still puts the incoming energy between
the two core maxima. Thus energy resolution and concentration in angular
momentum are physical inputs when asking whether the orbit discrepancy
can be observed as \(b\) shrinks. Refinement of a written polygon
provides neither.

\textbf{The two limits on the same position record.} For a trial reduced
action \(a>0\) compare the two Schrödinger Hamiltonians with kinetic
energy \(-a^2\Delta/(2m)\) and the displayed potentials. In core units
\(z=q/b\), \(s=t/t_b\), \(\xi=a/J_b\), and
\(w(z)=-V_{b,\pm}(b|z|)/(Ab^{3/2})\), the generator in
\(i\xi\partial_s\) is \(-\xi^2\Delta_z/2-w/2\). The exterior matching
radius \(2R/b\) is part of \(w\). The real-time kernel acquires a factor
\(b^{-3}\), canceled at each inserted position by \(d^3q=b^3d^3z\). The
launch coordinate and momentum magnitude become \(R/b\) and \(p_R/p_b\).
The clock reads \(T/t_b\) and the detector tolerances are \(\delta_x/b\)
and \(\delta_t/t_b\).
\href{groupoid-quantum-newton-states.md\#7-interacting-kernels-under-tangent-scaling}{Exact
propagator dilation, (20)--(22)} (passage).

The event is \(Q_1(T)>0\) in both. The unitary Duhamel estimate bounds
the absolute difference \(D(a,b)\) between event probabilities for
\emph{every} common incoming state by

\[
 D(a,b)\le \min\{1,\,3AT b^{3/2}/(8a)\}.
\]

Indeed the norm distance of the two evolved unit vectors bounds the
difference of any event probabilities, with purification covering mixed
inputs. A common clock jitter of size \(\delta_t\) replaces \(T\) by
\(T+\delta_t\) in the upper bound. To compare the two orders choose the
same family of incoming Gaussians in both. Each is centered at the
specified incoming state with component standard deviations
\(R\sqrt{a/(2J_R)}\) and \(p_R\sqrt{a/(2J_R)}\). For each fixed \(b\)
the forces are globally Lipschitz. The usual quadratic moment estimate
concentrates this packet on its Newton trajectory as \(a\downarrow0\).
In three dimensions the derivative of the summed moment
\(\langle|Q-q(t)|^2+|P-p(t)|^2/(m\omega)^2\rangle\) is bounded above by
\((\omega+\operatorname{Lip}(F)/(m\omega))\) times that moment for any
\(\omega>0\). Its initial value is \(O(a)\). The estimate is only being
used at \emph{fixed} \(b\).
\href{lipschitz-certificate-quantum-lift.md}{Lipschitz packet bounds,
§4} (passage). For all sufficiently small fixed \(b\) the two classical
endpoints stay strictly on opposite sides throughout the detector
window. The same conclusions hold with the stated bounded readout
errors, since the moment bound is uniform on that window. Thus

\[
 \lim_{b\downarrow0}\lim_{a\downarrow0}D(a,b)=1,\qquad
 \lim_{a\downarrow0}\lim_{b\downarrow0}D(a,b)=0 .
\]

Holding \(a>0\) fixed is sufficient for stability against the two core
changes. Stability also holds along joint paths with \(a\to0\) and
\(AT b^{3/2}/a\to0\), and under the classical flux bound above. No
positive lower bound on physical \(\hbar\) follows from either limiting
order. Here \(a/J_R\) is the dimensionless trial action,
\(h_P=2\pi\hbar\) if \(a=\hbar\), \(b\) is a source radius, and the time
mesh is a separate regulator. Semiclassical branch selection for rough
Hamiltonians has substantial prior art.
\href{https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.1651}{Athanassoulis and Paul}
(abstract) and
\href{cusp-quantum-preparation-selection.md}{cusp-preparation
comparison} (passage).

\textbf{Newtonian inverse-square attraction.} There is also a
finite-angle test for the actual Kepler force. Give two transparent
uniform source spheres the same total strength \(\kappa\) (energy times
length) and radii \(b\) and \(b/2\). Their exterior potential is
\(-\kappa/r\) and the interior force of a sphere of radius \(d\) is
\(-\kappa q/d^3\). These are Newton's spherical attraction results.
\href{https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Mathematical_Principles_of_Natural_Philosophy_\%281729\%29/Book_1/Section_12}{Motte
1729, Book I, Section XII, Propositions LXXIII--LXXIV} (passages). At
zero energy and \(L=\sqrt{m\kappa b}\) the point-mass periapsis is
\(b/2\). Take identical incoming data at a fixed exterior radius
\(R>b\). The Kepler parabola has \(r=b/(1+\cos\theta)\), so the narrow
source gives an angular advance \(\pi\) between the crossings of
\(r=b\).

For the wider source use \(b\) as length and \(t_K=\sqrt{mb^3/\kappa}\)
as time. In the orbital plane the interior solution from entry is
\((\cos s-\sin s,\sin s)\) since the inward and tangential velocity
components have unit magnitude in these units. It exits at
\(s=\arctan 2\) at \((-1,2)/\sqrt5\). Thus the two return angles at
\(R\) differ by exactly \(\arctan 2\), independent of \(b\). The times
inside \(r=b\) are \((4/3)t_K\) and \((\arctan 2)t_K\) for the narrow
and wide sources. Each inner action is \(O(\sqrt{m\kappa b})\). The
angular effect explains which part of an action estimate must survive
point insertion. For either orbit let
\(G(E,L)=2\int_{r_{\min}}^R\sqrt{2m(E-V(r))-L^2/r^2}\,dr\). At a simple
turning point the endpoint term vanishes and \(-\partial_L G\) is the
unwrapped angle between the two crossings of \(R\) along the orbit. The
inner contributions to \(G\) shrink as \(O(\sqrt{m\kappa b})\), but
their \(L\) derivatives differ by \(\arctan 2\) at the specified \(L\).
\href{../docs/classics/Hamilton_GeneralMethodDynamics_1834_Wilkins.md}{Hamilton,
1834, law of varying action} (passage).

Here the peak force grows as \(b^{-2}\) and the entry speed as
\(b^{-1/2}\). The affected disk of incoming flux has fraction \(b/R\)
for the Kepler exterior. This tests near-radial preparations as the
source shrinks. The \href{self-sufficiency-contrast.md}{Coulomb
propagation estimate} concerns fixed collision-free intervals. Smoothing
and the order of the angular-momentum and source limits have a
literature. \href{https://arxiv.org/abs/0905.1579}{Castelli and
Terracini} (abstract; their positive smoothing result concerns
logarithmic singularities).

\subsection{A three-energy test of central-force
trajectories}\label{a-three-energy-test-of-central-force-trajectories}

Newton's force measurement and an outgoing orbit direction give an
action difference in mechanical units. Consider two equal masses in a
nonrelativistic elastic collision. Write \(\mu\) for their reduced mass,
\(v\) for their incident relative speed, \(E=\mu v^2/2\), and
\(\kappa>0\) for the coefficient of the repulsive potential \(\kappa/r\)
(energy times length). With \(d=\kappa/(\mu v^2)\) the Rutherford orbit
gives the impact parameter \(b=d\cot(\theta/2)\) for the labelled
centre-of-mass deflection \(0<\theta<\pi\). The turning radius is
\(d+\sqrt{d^2+b^2}\ge 2d\). Any change of force confined strictly inside
\(2d\) leaves every such incoming classical orbit intact. Deflections
outside that region, including atomic screening or multiple collisions,
require an independent error bound.
\href{https://www.physics.utoronto.ca/~phy326/cc/Rutherford1911.pdf}{Rutherford,
1911, §2, pp.~672--674} (passage, applied to relative motion).

If the outgoing particles are unlabelled, the two possible angles are
\(\theta\) and \(\pi-\theta\). Their relative angular characteristic
action, set to zero at \(\pi/2\), has magnitude

\[
 |\Delta W|=\frac{2\kappa}{v}u,\qquad u=|\log\cot(\theta/2)|.
\]

Indeed Hamilton's endpoint variation gives the angular momentum as the
magnitude of the angular derivative. The two alternative derivatives
enter the difference with the same sign and their magnitudes sum to
\(\mu v d[\cot(\theta/2)+\tan(\theta/2)]=2\kappa/(v\sin\theta)\). The
difference uses a common large-radius reference, so the common radial
action constant drops out. This supplies a mechanical action coordinate
for the detector, before specifying any rule for phases.
\href{../docs/classics/Hamilton_GeneralMethodDynamics_1834_Wilkins.md}{Hamilton,
1834, law of varying action} (passage).

\textbf{Three settings on one incoming ensemble.} Count each collision
once and accept the full azimuth. With a target at rest the smaller of
the two laboratory fork angles is \(\alpha\le\pi/4\) and
\(u=\log\cot\alpha\). Choose a dimensionless angular displacement \(U\)
and a fixed width \(0<w<U\). Choose incident energies so that
\(d_\pm=d_0 e^{\pm U}\) (\(E_\pm=E_0e^{\mp U}\)). For any reference
length \(b_{\rm ref}>0\) set \(X=\log(b/b_{\rm ref})\) and
\(s_j=\log(d_j/b_{\rm ref})\). The classical observation at setting
\(j\) is exactly \(u_j=|X-s_j|\), including both possible assignments of
the outgoing particles. A head-on collision lies outside the finite
angular windows below.

Let \(H_w(y)=(1-|y|/w)_+\) and let \(Y_j[h]\) be the sum of the event
weights \(h(u)\) divided by the integrated luminosity in that run. For a
thin uniform target the latter is the incident particle count times its
areal number density, so \(Y_j\) has units of area. The incoming impact
measure per unit exposure may be arbitrary, provided it is common across
the three settings. Define

\[
 C=Y_+[H_w]+Y_-[H_w]-Y_0[H_w(\,\cdot-U)] .
\]

Then \textbf{\(C=0\) for Newton trajectories}. For each individual
\(X\), the two disjoint triangular windows centered at \(s_0\pm U\) sum
exactly to \(H_w(|X-s_0|-U)\). Integration proves the identity even for
point masses in the incoming law. The reference length cancels. It is
enough that \((X,\zeta)\) have one joint law when the actual Coulomb
length is \(d_je^\zeta\) in run \(j\). The same identity then holds
pointwise in \(X-\zeta\). Thus even a common fractional energy spread
correlated with impact parameter is allowed. A common error in the
absolute force strength also cancels. Time insertion leaves the
continuum deflection unchanged on these collision-free orbits.

The detector weight is Lipschitz with constant \(1/w\). If the measured
\(u_j\) differs from the ideal value by at most \(e_j\), the classical
residual obeys \(|C|\le M(e_++e_-+e_0)/w\). Here \(M\) is an upper bound
on the common impact measure of the union of the three preimages,
enlarged by their error margins. A discrepancy \(\epsilon_j\) between
the actual and common input measures, measured by the largest absolute
difference on weights between zero and one on that union, adds
\(\sum_j\epsilon_j\) to the bound. The \(\epsilon_j\) and \(M\) have
units of area. Uncertainties in efficiency, backgrounds, luminosity and
sampling enter in the units of \(C\). A detector distance and pixel
width set the angular error \(\delta\alpha\). On an acceptance enlarged
to \(\alpha\ge\alpha_{\min}>0\) within the folded half, its contribution
to \(e_j\) is at most \(2\delta\alpha/\sin(2\alpha_{\min})\). A flight
length \(\ell\) and an independent clock interval \(t\) give
\(s_j=\log(\kappa t^2/(\mu b_{\rm ref}\ell^2))\). Thus calibrated errors
in \(\ell,t\) propagate directly to \(s_j\). The clock and position
resolutions stay fixed while the mathematical time mesh shrinks.

Run normalization can also be eliminated for the classical test. At
three widths \(w_k<U\), let \(N_{jk}\) be the background- and
efficiency-corrected weighted counts with the above windows. If only the
three positive run exposures are unknown, the middle-setting row
\((N_{0k})_k\) must be a positive linear combination of the other two
rows with the same coefficients at all widths in the mean. The matrix of
mean counts therefore has determinant zero. This tests relative angular
shapes alone when the aperture response is known.

\textbf{How accurately must the force be known?} Write the actual radial
force on a scattering orbit as \(\kappa[1+g(r)]/r^2\), positive
outwards. Measure the polar angle \(\phi\) from the incoming asymptote
and let \(\Phi=\pi-\theta\) at exit. For \(b>0\) and a repulsive
scattering orbit with \(0<\Phi<\pi\), Binet's equation for \(y=1/r\) is
\(y''+y=-(d/b^2)[1+g(r)]\), with \(y(0)=0\), \(y'(0)=1/b\). At the exit
\(y(\Phi)=0\). The sine Green function gives

\[
 \frac{b}{d}\tan(\theta/2)=1+\bar g,\qquad
 \bar g=\frac{\int_0^\Phi\sin(\Phi-\phi)\,g(r(\phi))\,d\phi}
                    {1-\cos\Phi}.
\]

This is a positive weighted average of the actual force error along the
orbit. It requires no bound on a force gradient. An independent bound
\(|\bar g|\le\gamma<1\) adds at most \(-\log(1-\gamma)\) to the error
\(e_j\) above. A bound on \(|g|\) from short-time force measurements on
the traversed annulus suffices if the tails are also controlled. An
outer portion of total polar angle \(h\) with \(|g|\le D\) contributes
at most \(Dh/(1-\cos\Phi)\) to the average. This allows a screened tail
to be treated separately.
\href{https://teorica.fis.ucm.es/artemio/Notas\%20de\%20curso/CM.pdf}{Binet's
equation, \emph{Motion in a central potential}, §2} (passage).

There is a direct threshold for an ideal counter accepting
\(0\le u\le w\) with full azimuth, uniform incident fluence and one
fixed speed. For any ray it accepts, the force-error identity confines
\(b\) to \([d(1-\gamma)e^{-w},d(1+\gamma)e^w]\). Dividing the area of
this annulus by the unperturbed aperture cross section gives

\[
 \frac{Y_{\rm actual}}{Y_{\rm Coulomb}}
 \le 1+\gamma^2+2\gamma\coth(2w).
\]

For a twofold excess one therefore needs
\(\gamma\ge 1/(\sqrt{1+\coth^2(2w)}+\coth(2w))\). For
\(w=\log\cot40^\circ\) this is about \(0.16\). The statement is
independent of a phase law and of the mathematical time partition. It
bounds the largest orbit-averaged force error on accepted rays if force
alone is to account for the ideal counter's excess. A real counter needs
its measured angular response in place of the ideal window.

\textbf{What an excess measures.} Take the established plane-wave Mott
cross section for identical spin-zero particles with uniform incident
fluence and only elastic Coulomb scattering. A finite beam requires
calibration of the incident directions, coherence and detector response
for this law to apply. In terms of a trial reduced action \(a>0\) and
\(\eta_j=\kappa/(a v_j)\), its full-azimuth cross section on the folded
half-sphere is

\[
 dY_j=4\pi d_j^2
       [\cosh(2u)+\cos(2\eta_j u)]\,du,\qquad u\ge0 .
\]

The first term is the sum of the two classical contributions. The second
is the exchange term, with argument \(|\Delta W|/a\).
\href{https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1930.0006}{Mott, 1930} (article
metadata) and \href{https://arxiv.org/html/1402.1858\#S3}{Canto, Hussein
and Mittig, §III, Eqs. (9)--(10)} (formula passages). At a \emph{single}
energy the bounded nonnegative relative classical illumination

\[
 1+\frac{\cos(k\log(b/d))}{\cosh(2\log(b/d))}
\]

multiplies both branches by the same factor. Choosing the dimensionless
frequency \(k=2\eta_j\) reproduces the entire angular distribution.
Independence of the target positions can exclude such a reweighting
physically. For example, translate one collision partner independently
and uniformly over a transverse area \(A\). Conditional on an arbitrary
other impact point, the probability for the relative offset to lie in a
set \(B\) is \(|B|/|A|\) whenever its translated preimage is contained
in \(A\). This guard condition suffices for the finite accepted annuli.
A homogeneous gas independent of the incident beam gives the same area
weighting in the thin-target approximation. The three-energy identity
also covers a common nonuniform illumination.

With \(S_j=\sin(\eta_j w)/(\eta_j w)\), integration against the three
specified triangular weights gives

\[
 C_a=2\pi w\big[d_+^2 S_+^2+d_-^2 S_-^2
                 -2d_0^2 S_0^2\cos(2\eta_0 U)\big] .
\]

The half-window cosine integral at the origin is \(w S_j^2/2\) and the
full translated one is \(w S_0^2\cos(2\eta_0 U)\). Retaining a finite
\(w\) is essential. If \(|C_{\rm obs}|-\rho=C_{\rm low}>0\), where
\(\rho\) bounds all errors relative to this ideal prediction at the
asserted confidence level, \(|\sin|\le1\) gives the model bound

\[
 a\ \ge\ \kappa\sqrt{\frac{w C_{\rm low}}
 {2\pi(d_+^2v_+^2+d_-^2v_-^2+2d_0^2v_0^2)}} .
\]

For a common incoherent mixture of relative speeds as above, average
each \(d_j^2v_j^2\) over the measured spread, assuming these moments are
finite and the uniform-fluence law applies at each speed. The angular
settings and the energy preparation in this bound are fixed
independently of \(a\). With \(U=\log4\) and monochromatic runs, for
example, \(a_1=\kappa w/(\pi v_0)\) gives \(C_{a_1}=w d_0^2/(2\pi)\),
whereas \(a_1/2\) gives zero. Moreover \(a_1/2\) and \(a_1/4\) give
exactly the same three weighted yields as the classical uniform beam,
since all three sine factors vanish for both. These examples distinguish
rejection of the classical prediction from unique identification of a
positive action.

The separation of \(a_1\) from \(a_1/2\) survives a finite incoming
speed band with the same detector. Let the actual speeds be
\(\lambda v_j\) with a common distribution of
\(|\lambda-1|\le\delta_v=1/100\). For an integer \(n>0\),
\(|\sin(n\pi/\lambda)/(n\pi/\lambda)|\le\delta_v\), while
\(\sin(\pi/(2\lambda))/(\pi/(2\lambda))>1/2\) throughout this band. At
\(a_1\) the minus-setting factor therefore exceeds \(1/2\) and the
middle one is at most \(\delta_v\) in magnitude. At \(a_1/2\) all three
are at most \(\delta_v\). Since the squared length ratios are
\(16,1,1/16\) and each \(d_j^2\) acquires the same factor
\(\lambda^{-4}\), the difference of the two mean contrasts is at least
\(2\pi w d_0^2(1/64-21\delta_v^2)/(1+\delta_v)^4>0\). This leaves a
positive allowance for calibration error at fixed clock and angular
resolution. The exact equality of the other pair above was for
monochromatic runs.

\textbf{What was actually measured.} Chadwick used a helium gas target,
graphite diaphragms admitting laboratory angles from \(40\) to \(50\)
degrees, and argon runs at matched stopping power. He reported an
observed-to-classical ratio near two at the lowest speeds. The argon
standard assumes Coulomb scattering, and the different particle energies
at the screen could reduce helium counting efficiency in the two slowest
runs. This is evidence for a finite-aperture excess independent of
fitting a Mott amplitude. For any \emph{fixed} integrable angular
response, its exchange contribution vanishes as \(a\downarrow0\) by the
Riemann--Lebesgue lemma, also after averaging a fixed speed distribution
with integrable scattering weight. Thus an independently calibrated
excess in that aperture already excludes the zero limit within the Mott
family. Chadwick's apparatus had its own angular response.
\href{https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1930.0098}{Chadwick, 1930,
pp.~116--121} (\href{https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.27026}{volume
scan}, passages read in the library OCR) and
\href{https://www.iitg.ac.in/rksri/MA746\%20Fourier\%20Analysis\%20Lecture\%20notes\%202025.pdf}{Riemann--Lebesgue
lemma, Theorem 2.13} (statement).

Blackett and Champion recorded both outgoing arms for 428 forks among
56,820 photographed tracks. Their range and angle cuts and their
geometrical identification of helium events can be checked against the
cloud-chamber preparation. They report a discrepancy statistic of 15.3
for the classical and 1.04 for the Mott expectations in the upper six
cells. Their cell boundaries used the Mott phase, and they report missed
short spurs in the lower cells. Heydenburg and Temmer later measured
precise relative angular distributions below 400 keV. Their absolute
normalization below 2 MeV used a fitted scattering phase shift. None of
these published summaries gives the three matched weighted yields or
their calibration errors. A numerical value of \(C_{\rm low}\) for this
test remains open.
\href{https://doi.org/10.1098/rspa.1931.0011}{Blackett and Champion,
1931, pp.~382--388}
(\href{https://archive.org/details/dli.ernet.7436}{volume scan},
passages read in the library OCR) and
\href{https://journals.aps.org/pr/abstract/10.1103/PhysRev.104.123}{Heydenburg
and Temmer, 1956} (abstract).

\subsection{An exterior force test with
argon}\label{an-exterior-force-test-with-argon}

\textbf{6.1 Sol Max, 2026-11-18.} At \(0.34\) eV Buckman and Lohmann
report an electron--argon attenuation cross section of \(0.318\) square
ångströms (relative error \(3.5\%\), one standard deviation). The
question from Lemma X is whether a separately measured radial force
could produce so little loss. The following comparison uses elastic
trajectories and a physical exit aperture. All assumptions about the
atom's interior can be dropped once the contributing rays are shown to
turn outside it.
\href{https://doi.org/10.1088/0022-3700/19/16/013}{Buckman and Lohmann,
§§2--4 and Table 2} (passages and data row).

\textbf{The independently measurable force coefficient.} Let
\(\alpha_v\) be the static dipole polarizability expressed as a volume,
so \(\mathbf p_{\rm ind}=4\pi\epsilon_0\alpha_v\mathbf E\). The
induced-dipole energy in a charge's field is \(-C_4/r^4\) to leading
order, with \(C_4=[e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)]\alpha_v/2\). Dielectric
measurements on argon give a molar polarizability
\(A_\epsilon=4.140686(10)\) cubic centimetres per mole. Using
\(\alpha_v=3A_\epsilon/(4\pi N_A)\) and the electrostatic coupling
\(1.44\) eV nm gives \(C_4\simeq1.18\,10^{-3}\) eV nm\(^4\). These
inputs can be calibrated by charge, force, length and particle number. A
dielectric measurement probes a weak, nearly uniform field. It
determines the coefficient at large distance. The accuracy of the
instantaneous radial force in an actual flyby is an additional
measurement.
\href{https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.120.123203}{Gaiser and
Fellmuth, Table III} (method and data passage).

\textbf{A finite annulus with a known minimum deflection.} Let
\(E=\mu v^2/2\) be the relative kinetic energy, \(\mu\) the reduced
mass, and \(p=\mu v\). Write \(V(\infty)=0\) and assume a
time-independent central potential, locally \(C^2\) outside \(r_0\).
Suppose that on \(r\ge r_0\) its inward force magnitude obeys \[
 4(1-\gamma)C_4/r^5\ \le V'(r)\le\ 4(1+\gamma)C_4/r^5,
 \qquad 0\le\gamma<1 .
\] Let \(n(r)=\sqrt{1-V(r)/E}\), \(\rho=r n(r)\) and
\(B(r)=rV'(r)/[2(E-V(r))]\). Then \(d\log\rho/d\log r=1-B(r)\). Fix
impact radii \(b_1\le b\le b_2\). If \[
 B_*=\frac{2(1+\gamma)C_4}{E r_0^4}<\tfrac12,\qquad
 b_1>r_0\sqrt{1+\frac{(1+\gamma)C_4}{E r_0^4}},
\] then \(0\le B\le B_*\) outside \(r_0\) and the turning point
\(\rho(r)=b\) lies in this exterior region. Use the usual central-orbit
quadrature, with \(\chi\) positive for inward bending and
\(\rho=b\sec u\). Subtracting its free angle gives \[
 \chi(b)=2\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{B(r(b\sec u))}{1-B(r(b\sec u))}\,du .
\] Here \(r(\rho)\) is the unique exterior inverse. This is the
single-turning-point formula of
\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0605637}{Hendi, Henn and Leonhardt,
Eqs. (1)--(4)} (passage, opposite sign convention). Since \(n\ge1\) and
\(B\ge 2(1-\gamma)C_4 n^2/(E\rho^4)\), the integral of \(\cos^4u\) gives
the finite bounds \[
 \frac{3\pi(1-\gamma)C_4}{4E b^4}
 \ \le\ \chi(b)\ \le\ \frac{\pi B_*}{1-B_*}<\pi .
\] The upper bound excludes a return to the forward detector after a
full turn. Neither bound needs the force inside \(r_0\). Inserting times
into a convergent trajectory calculation leaves both inequalities
intact.

\textbf{Putting the loss in the units of the experiment.} Take
\(E\in[0.30,0.40]\) eV, \(C_4\in[1.15,1.20]\,10^{-3}\) eV nm\(^4\),
\(\gamma=1/2\), \(r_0=0.50\) nm and \([b_1,b_2]=[0.55,0.60]\) nm.
Throughout this band \(B_*\le0.192\), \(\rho(r_0)<0.525\) nm and
\(\chi(b)>0.026\) rad. The annulus has area
\(A=\pi(b_2^2-b_1^2)=0.0575\pi\) nm\(^2\), or \(18.06\) square
ångströms. Uniformly distributed independent gas atoms give this area
per unit target column for any fixed incident ray.

Buckman and Lohmann used a field-free tube of length \(L=255\) mm with a
\(D=2\) mm diameter exit. For a collision a distance \(z\) upstream, the
undeflected and deflected rays can both cross that exit only if their
angle is at most \(D/z\). The angle between the two unit directions is
bounded by their transverse slope difference, which is at most the
diameter of the disk divided by \(z\). Thus for the upstream half the
angle is at most \(2D/L<0.016\) rad. This uses only straight-line flight
and the measured lengths. In a calibration allowing up to \(0.004\) rad
in total for the relative-to-laboratory angle, residual fields,
alignment and finite-distance propagation, take \(\theta=0.020\) rad as
the largest accepted relative deflection there. The margin is positive
at fixed length and clock resolution. The authors estimate an energy
resolution below \(20\) meV at \(0.4\) eV. A resolution width alone
gives no hard support for the energy distribution.
\href{https://doi.org/10.1088/0022-3700/19/16/013}{Buckman and Lohmann,
pp.~2548--2550, 2553--2556, 2561} (instrument and resolution passages).

Let \(Y\) be the gas-induced loss per unit column in the dilute limit,
normalized to the empty-cell detected current. Assume its linear term is
the single-atom loss minus the single-atom gain. A column scan checks
the range in which the measured slope applies. Write \(f\) for a
certified lower fraction of reference fluence and gas column meeting the
energy, upstream-location and angular conditions, including the relative
flux for moving targets. More precisely, on the annulus these good
encounters dominate \(fP(dE)\,2\pi b\,db\) per unit column for a
probability \(P\) on the specified energy band. Let \(G\) bound, in area
units, the gain from rays entering the counted output that would
otherwise have missed it, including migration between timing bins. All
good encounters in that annulus remove a reference count. The Newton
prediction therefore obeys \[
 Y_{\rm Newton}\ge f A-G .
\] For \(f=1/2\) and \(G=0\) this exceeds \(9.0\) square ångströms. Even
a conservative \textbf{trial} upper value \(Y_{\rm hi}=0.40\) square
ångströms would suffice if \(G=0\) and \(f>0.023\). Ferch et
al.~independently measured \(0.311\) square ångströms at \(0.345\) eV
with a magnetic lens. Its angular response differs from that of the
field-free tube. Both experiments discuss forward-scattering corrections
using angular distributions. Their quoted errors do not by themselves
bound \(G\) under an alternative classical angular law. Pressure scans,
column density, empty-cell fluence, background and detector efficiency
enter the comparison.
\href{https://doi.org/10.1088/0022-3700/18/5/017}{Ferch et al., Table 1
and §§3.1, 4} and
\href{https://doi.org/10.1088/0022-3700/19/16/013}{Buckman and Lohmann,
§4} (data and correction passages).

A way to control the gain independently is to admit only rays through
two upstream stops of radius \(a_s=0.25\) mm a distance \(L_s=L\) apart,
the second at the cell entrance. Every straight ray through both would
arrive within \(a_s(1+2L/L_s)=0.75\) mm of the exit centre. Pulse gates
at the stops separated by \(0.74\) microseconds, each with a half-width
of \(5\) ns, select velocity using \(L_s\) and a clock. The on-axis
flight interval of \(0.73\)--\(0.75\) microseconds is inside the
declared energy band for electrons on stationary argon. The small
inclination, mass ratio, gas temperature and timing tails enter \(f\).
Count the whole outgoing pulse with calibrated efficiency across the
exit. A wide time gate and an empty-cell beam profile are essential to
the bound on \(G\). This is a proposed preparation. The two published
experiments did not report these guarded counts.

\textbf{What could calibrate the force independently?} The nominal
traversal time \(r_0/v\) is \(1.3\)--\(1.6\) fs. Spatial and temporal
response can be tested with different external fields. The following two
estimates specify what such measurements would control.

First consider a neutral spherical source whose static polarization
energy in an imposed potential is minus one half a positive, rotation-
invariant quadratic form. Suppose the responding charge is contained
inside \(r_1=0.40\) nm and the form is continuous on the converging
exterior point-charge expansion there (for example the support is
strictly smaller than \(r_1\) and the form is bounded on its
potentials). The different spherical harmonic degrees are orthogonal for
this form. Neutrality removes degree zero. Consequently the static
energy is \(V_{\rm st}(r)=-\sum_{\ell\ge1}c_\ell/r^{2\ell+2}\), with
\(c_\ell\ge0\) and \(c_1=C_4\). Convergence at \(r_1\) suffices to
differentiate at \(r\ge r_0>r_1\). Zhang et al.~distinguish a convergent
exterior polarization series for confined hydrogen from the asymptotic
free-atom expansion. \href{https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4705279}{Zhang et
al.~(2012)} (abstract).

A single measurement of the work of bringing a charge from infinity to
\(r_1\) now bounds the higher-order force on the whole annulus. Put
\(u_1=-r_1^4V_{\rm st}(r_1)/C_4-1\). Each higher multipole contributes a
nonnegative weight to \(u_1\). Its contribution to the fractional force
excess at \(r\) equals that weight times
\((\ell+1)(r_1/r)^{2\ell-2}/2\). For \(\ell\ge2\) these factors are at
most \((3/2)(0.40/0.50)^2=0.96\) because their successive ratios are
bounded by \((4/3)(0.40/0.50)^2<1\). Thus \[
 0\le \frac{r^5 V'_{\rm st}(r)}{4C_4}-1\le0.96\,u_1,\qquad r\ge r_0 .
\] For example, an independent upper limit \(u_1\le1/4\) would leave a
fractional allowance of \(0.26\) before the upper force tolerance
\(1.50\) is reached. Penetration by the probe, higher powers of field
strength and delayed response require their own control for real argon.

For a temporal check, prescribe a straight probe passage
\(\mathbf r(t)=(b,0,vt)\) independently of the scattering experiment.
Write \(K=|e|/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\), \(\mathbf E(t)=K\mathbf r/r^3\) up to
overall sign, and \(\alpha=4\pi\epsilon_0\alpha_v\). Model the
\emph{dipole} by passive classical oscillator modes with positive static
weights \(d\alpha(\nu)\) summing to \(\alpha\), angular frequencies
\(\nu>0\) and zero incoming excitation. Each mode of static weight \(a\)
obeys \(\ddot{\mathbf d}+\nu^2\mathbf d=a\nu^2\mathbf E(t)\). All
choices of \(\nu\) give the same static response per unit weight. At
closest approach the retarded solution gives
\(|\mathbf d(0)|/(a|\mathbf E(0)|)\le\pi\nu b/(2v)\) by integrating
\(K/(b^2+v^2t^2)\) over the incoming half-line. An arbitrarily slow
classical dipole therefore defeats a static force calibration even when
its whole static work curve is known.

Conversely let \(\tau_{\rm pol}=\alpha^{-1}\int\nu^{-1}\,d\alpha(\nu)\).
For the integrated force on the prescribed probe, the error from using
an instantaneous dipole obeys \[
 \frac{|\mathbf I_{\rm ret}-\mathbf I_{\rm stat}|}{|\mathbf I_{\rm stat}|}
 \le \frac{8v\tau_{\rm pol}}{b},\qquad
 |\mathbf I_{\rm stat}|=\frac{3\pi\alpha K^2}{4vb^4}.
\] In fact \(\mathbf d-a\mathbf E\) is the retarded sine integral of
\(-a\ddot{\mathbf E}/\nu\). Along the straight line,
\(\|\nabla\mathbf E(t)\|\le2K/r(t)^3\) and
\(|\ddot{\mathbf E}(s)|\le6Kv^2/r(s)^4\). For the latter, the squared
dimensionless coefficient is \(45x^4-18x^2+9\le36\) for
\(x=\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\hat{\mathbf v}\). The integral of these two
even bounds over \(s<t\) is half their full product integral, namely
\(6\pi K^2/b^5\). Integration of the static transverse force and summing
the retarded errors over modes gives the displayed ratio. Masses enter
through \(\mu^{-1}=m_e^{-1}+M_{\rm Ar}^{-1}\) when converting \(E\) to
\(v\).

The frequencies in this moment are clock frequencies. For this mode
model the causal susceptibility has
\(d\alpha(\nu)=(2/\pi)\operatorname{Im}\alpha(\nu+i0)\,d\nu/\nu\),
including line masses. Thus an independently calibrated absorption
spectrum can bound \(\tau_{\rm pol}\) without converting photon energy
to frequency with \(\hbar\). A bound \(\tau_{\rm pol}\le9\,10^{-17}\) s
would put the integrated dipole error below one half throughout the test
annulus. The prominent argon lines near \(105\) and \(107\) nm have
inverse angular frequencies near \(5.6\,10^{-17}\) s via
\(\lambda/(2\pi c)\). Their positions and strengths alone do not bound
the whole positive moment. The straight-probe estimate controls a
prescribed path. The coupled calculation below allows the probe to turn
and the target to retain energy.
\href{https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.49.2363}{Ligtenberg et al.,
optical absorption measurements} (abstract) and
\href{https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/Handbook/Tables/argontable3.htm}{NIST,
Ar I resonance wavelengths} (table).

There is a numerical cross-check of the required timescale. Kumar and
Thakkar tabulate argon oscillator-strength moments \(S_{-2}=11.08\) and
\(S_{-3}=16.75\) in atomic units. Static dipole weights are proportional
to oscillator strengths divided by squared frequency. Their ratio
therefore gives \(\tau_{\rm pol}=(16.75/11.08)t_{\rm at}\simeq
3.66\,10^{-17}\) s with the conventional atomic time
\(t_{\rm at}=\hbar/E_{\rm h}\) (\(E_{\rm h}\) is the Hartree energy). It
puts \(8v\tau_{\rm pol}/b\) near \(0.20\) at the fastest, closest
straight probe in the band. Their distribution combines absorption and
refractivity data with theoretical input and a sum-rule constraint. For
an independent test of action necessity the frequency integral must be
calibrated with lengths, clocks and field response, with spectral
coverage stated. \href{https://doi.org/10.1063/1.3315418}{Kumar and
Thakkar, §§II--III and Table I} (method and moment table).

\subsubsection{Two pulse tests for a freely moving
charge}\label{two-pulse-tests-for-a-freely-moving-charge}

\textbf{6.1 Sol Max, 2026-11-18.} Consider the reciprocal classical
model defined below, with its internal modes initially at rest. Given
the specified static work and two pulse readings, every incoming
electron with relative energy \(0.33\)--\(0.35\) eV and impact radius
\(0.70\)--\(0.72\) nm turns by more than \(0.0133\) rad. In the first
tenth of the field-free cell the largest accepted turn is \(0.0128\) rad
under the angular calibration above. The claim allows energy and angular
momentum transfer to the target and modes arbitrarily close to zero
frequency.

\textbf{What is measured.} Retain the static exterior expansion above
and write \(w_1=1\), \(w_\ell=c_\ell/(C_4 r_1^{2\ell-2})\) and
\(U=\sum_{\ell\ge2}w_\ell=-r_1^4 V_{\rm st}(r_1)/C_4-1\). Each
\(c_\ell\) has units of energy times length\(^{2\ell+2}\). The dipole
coefficient is measured in a uniform field. The equilibrium work of a
point charge at \(r_1\) supplies \(1+U\). Use the same source position
for the static and pulsed readings. The estimates below allow
\(r_1=0.400\pm0.001\) nm. In each angular degree assume a normalized
positive distribution \(P_\ell(d\nu)\) of undamped oscillator modes with
angular frequencies \(\nu>0\). Use the same linear, isotropic response
to the imposed fields and the passing charge at the stated amplitudes.
The equilibrium references must include any slowly relaxing response.
For a bounded unit drive of duration \(T\) starting from rest, an
additional mode of static weight \(w\) changes the finite response by at
most \(w(\nu T)^2/2\), since \(|\nu\sin(\nu t)|\le\nu^2t\) for
\(t\ge0\). Its equilibrium contribution is \(w\). Finite pulse readings
alone cannot certify that upper bound.

Apply an exponential envelope of rate \(\sigma=2.5\,10^{14}\) s\(^{-1}\)
to two spatial profiles. The first imposes a uniform field and reads the
induced dipole along it. The second imposes the Coulomb potential of a
charge at \(r_1\) and reads the induced potential there. At full
amplitude take the electron charge magnitude \(|e|\) and a uniform field
of magnitude \(|e|/(4\pi\epsilon_0 r_1^2)\), about \(9\) V/nm. The
point-charge profile specifies the external field over the responding
region. Each readout is conjugate to the applied amplitude and is
oriented to give a positive static dipole contribution. Normalize the
induced potential by its equilibrium dipole magnitude
\(2C_4/(|e|r_1^4)\), about \(0.092\) V. A mode driven from the remote
past by \(e^{\sigma t}\) follows with factor \(\nu^2/(\nu^2+\sigma^2)\).
Thus the normalized static reading minus the pulse reading gives \(d_1\)
for the uniform profile and \(d_1+d_H\) for the point-charge profile,
where \[
 h_\sigma(\nu)=\frac{\sigma^2}{\nu^2+\sigma^2},\qquad
 d_1=\int h_\sigma\,dP_1,\qquad
 d_H=\sum_{\ell\ge2}w_\ell\int h_\sigma\,dP_\ell .
\] Both pulses may start at \(t=-17/\sigma\) from rest. Integrate the
signal and the envelope over the last \(1/\sigma\) before \(t=0\) and
divide the former by the latter. The omitted past contributes at most
\(e^{-17}\nu/\sqrt{\nu^2+\sigma^2}\) per unit static weight at any time
in the gate, by integration of the oscillator's sine kernel. The
envelope throughout the gate is at least \(e^{-1}\), so the normalized
error is at most \(e^{-16}\) per unit weight. Errors of both readings
enter the subtraction for \(d_H\). The duration is \(68\) fs and the
gate is \(4\) fs. The following trial upper limits include those errors
and the uncertainties in the spatial profiles, drive amplitudes, readout
gains and static references. \[
 U\le1/8,\qquad d_1\le10^{-4},\qquad d_H\le1.2\,10^{-5}.
\] The last limit corresponds to about \(1.1\) microvolts after dipole
subtraction at the nominal radius and \(C_4\). Two targets can have the
same static work at every radius and the same complete dipole response
while differing in the frequency of a quadrupole of positive weight. The
second pulse distinguishes them.

There is a check on the size of these proposed readings. In atomic units
put \(\epsilon_i\) equal to a tabulated excitation energy,
\(u=\sigma t_{\rm at}\) with \(t_{\rm at}=2.42\,10^{-17}\) s, and
\(\alpha_\ell=\sum_i f_i^{(\ell)}/\epsilon_i^2\). The effective spectrum
of Jiang et al.~then gives the degree-\(\ell\) deficit per unit weight
as
\(u^2\sum_i f_i^{(\ell)}/[\epsilon_i^2(\epsilon_i^2+u^2)]/\alpha_\ell\).
Their argon entries \(\alpha_1=11.08\), \(\alpha_2=52.80\),
\(\alpha_3=536.4\) give \(w_2\simeq0.083\) and \(w_3\simeq0.015\) using
\(w_\ell=(\alpha_\ell/\alpha_1)(a_0/r_1)^{2\ell-2}\) and the Bohr radius
\(a_0=0.0529\) nm. Their Table 2 gives \(d_1\simeq9.2\,10^{-5}\) and a
combined quadrupole and octupole contribution to \(d_H\) of about
\(8.7\,10^{-6}\). These are fitted atomic-unit benchmarks. The higher
degrees, charge penetration, equilibrium work and a clocked response at
the proposed field strength still need independent calibration.
\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/1108.0150}{Zhang et al., Eq. (1)}
(charge-potential convention) and
\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.7957}{Jiang et al., Eqs. (2)--(4),
§3.21 and Ar rows of Tables 1--2} (method and table passages).

\textbf{Coupled motion.} Put \(x=\mathbf r/b\), \(s=v(t-t_*)/b\),
\(\beta=r_1/b\) and \(q=C_4/(Eb^4)\), with \(E=\mu v^2/2\). Let
\(f_\ell(x)=|x|^{-\ell-1}\mathbf Y_\ell(\hat x)\), using real spherical
harmonics normalized by \(\mathbf Y_\ell(n)\cdot\mathbf Y_\ell(n')=
P_\ell^{\rm Leg}(n\cdot n')\), the Legendre polynomial. Primes here
denote \(s\) derivatives. Each target coordinate \(y_{\ell\nu}\) is
driven by the actual charge position. The reciprocal equations are \[
 \begin{aligned}
 y_{\ell\nu}''+\omega^2y_{\ell\nu}&=\omega^2 f_\ell(x),\qquad
        \omega=\nu b/v,\\
 x''&=q\sum_{\ell\ge1}w_\ell\beta^{2\ell-2}
        Df_\ell(x)^T\int y_{\ell\nu}\,dP_\ell .
 \end{aligned}
\] Indeed the mode potential is proportional to
\(|y|^2/2-y\cdot f_\ell(x)\) with the displayed positive weight and mode
kinetic term \(|y'|^2/(2\omega^2)\). Equilibrium gives \(y=f_\ell\) and
the measured static potential. Classical oscillator models of induced
dipoles are established. The model here assumes the convergent exterior
response of the preceding section, no incoming excitations and no
additional force on the exterior orbit.
\href{https://doi.org/10.1063/1.1589749}{Lamoureux and Roux} (abstract,
dipole model) and \href{https://dlmf.nist.gov/14.30.E9}{NIST DLMF
14.30.9} (harmonic addition formula).

Split the modes at \(3\sigma\). Their total static weight on the slow
side is \(S=\sum w_\ell P_\ell(\nu\le3\sigma)\le10(d_1+d_H)<0.0012\). On
the fast side \(F=\{\nu>3\sigma\}\), \(\nu^{-2}\le
10h_\sigma/(9\sigma^2)\). Write
\(\tau_{\ell,F}=\int_F\nu^{-1}\,dP_\ell\). Cauchy--Schwarz, including
the weights in the higher degrees, gives \[
 \begin{aligned}
 T_F&=\tau_{1,F}+1.85\sum_{\ell\ge2}w_\ell\tau_{\ell,F}\\
 &\le\frac{\sqrt{10d_1/9}+1.85\sqrt{10U d_H/9}}{\sigma}
 <5.18\,10^{-17}\ {\rm s}.
 \end{aligned}
\] Here \(v<3.52\,10^5\) m/s for electron--argon relative motion,
\(\Lambda=vT_F/b<0.0261\), and the preceding \(C_4\) band gives
\(0.0122<q<0.0152\). In mechanical units one needs \(C_4/\mu\) in
length\(^6\)/time\(^2\), since \(q=2(C_4/\mu)/(v^2b^4)\). This allows
force per unit reduced mass, lengths and flight times to set the
comparison without a photon energy--frequency conversion. The possibly
divergent inverse moment of the slow modes never enters the orbit
estimate.

Compare the moving charge with the static orbit \(X\) of the same
incoming impact, velocity and asymptotic time offset. Write
\(F_{\rm st}(x)\) for the right hand side at equilibrium, \(r=|X|\) and
\(g(s)=\sqrt{1+s^2}\). Energy and angular momentum give
\(|X'|^2=1+q\sum w_\ell\beta^{2\ell-2}r^{-2\ell-2}\),
\(|X\times X'|=1\). At \(r=0.99\) the squared value of \(r|X'|\) would
be at most \(0.99^2+q(1+U\,0.58^2)/0.99^2<1\). Thus \(r>0.99\),
\(\beta/r<0.58\) and \(|X'|<1.01\). Also \[
 (r^2/2)''=1-q\sum_{\ell\ge1}\ell w_\ell\beta^{2\ell-2}r^{-2\ell-2}
 \ge 1-q(1+2U\,0.58^2)/0.99^4>0.98 .
\] Choose \(t_*\) at the unique static turning point. Twice integrating
gives \(r\ge0.989g\). The single-turn quadrature above gives
\(3\pi q/4\le\chi_X<\pi\) since the dipole is present, all the extra
forces are inward and \(B(r)<2.3q/0.99^4<0.04\) along this orbit.

\textbf{The error in the outgoing angle.} For matching incoming
asymptotes let \[
 D(s)=\sup_{t\le s}\max\bigl\{|x(t)-X(t)|/g(t),\,|x'(t)-X'(t)|\bigr\}.
\] While \(D\le0.028\) the segment between the two positions lies at
radius at least \(a g\) with \(a=0.96\), and \(|x'|\le V=1.04\). On that
segment \(|F_{\rm st}|\le F_0 q a^{-5}g^{-5}\), \(F_0=2.3\), and
\(\|DF_{\rm st}\|\le12q a^{-6}g^{-6}\). Indeed \(\beta/a<0.60\).
Relative to the static dipole, a weight \(w_\ell\) adds at most
\((\ell+1)0.36^{\ell-1}/2\) times its weight to the force bound and
\((\ell+1)(2\ell+3)0.36^{\ell-1}/10\) times its weight to the derivative
bound. Both maxima for \(\ell\ge2\) occur at \(\ell=2\).

For the time delay, denote \(L(s)=|x''-F_{\rm st}(x)|\). Along the path
the oscillator solution gives \(y-f=-\int_{-\infty}^s
\cos(\omega(s-t))\dot f(t)\,dt\). For a fast mode integrate once more to
get \(|y-f|\le\omega^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^s|\ddot f(t)|\,dt\). Use the
first formula on slow modes. At radius \(R\),
\(\|Df_\ell\|\le(\ell+1)R^{-\ell-2}\) and
\(|D^2f_\ell[u,u]|\le H_\ell |u|^2R^{-\ell-3}\). Here \(H_1=6\) from the
dipole formula above and for \(\ell\ge2\) one may take
\(H_\ell=\sqrt{(\ell+1)(2\ell+1)(\ell+2)(2\ell+3)}\). For the first
bound the radial derivative has norm \((\ell+1)R^{-\ell-2}\) and is
orthogonal to the angular derivatives. The squared gradient trace is
\(\Delta |f_\ell|^2/2\) by harmonicity. The remaining tangential trace
is \(\ell(\ell+1)R^{-2\ell-4}\). The squared Frobenius norm of the
Hessian is \(\Delta^2|f_\ell|^2/4\), giving \(H_\ell\). In the product
with the force derivative the factor relative to the dipole is
\((\ell+1)H_\ell\,0.36^{\ell-1}/12<1.85\) for \(\ell\ge2\). It equals
\(3\sqrt{420}\,0.36/12\) at degree two. At degrees three and above use
\(H_\ell\le2(\ell+1)(\ell+2)\) and the decreasing product. For two first
derivatives the factor is \((\ell+1)^2
0.36^{\ell-1}/4\le1\).

Put \(k_1=12\Lambda a^{-7}\), \(k_2=4\Lambda a^{-6}\) and
\(k_0=4S a^{-6}\). The preceding product bounds give
\(L(s)\le qg(s)^{-3}K(s)\), where a nonnegative majorant is the solution
of \[
 \begin{aligned}
 K(s)&=\int_{-\infty}^s [\,A(t)+k_2qg(t)^{-6}K(t)\,]\,dt,\\
 A&=k_1V^2g^{-4}+k_0Vg^{-3}+k_2 F_0 q a^{-5}g^{-8}.
 \end{aligned}
\] The three terms in \(A\) come from \(D^2f[x',x']\) on fast modes,
\(Df\,x'\) on slow modes and \(Df\,F_{\rm st}\) on fast modes. The
remaining \(Df(x''-F_{\rm st})\) is the integral feedback above. All
sums are absolutely bounded on the exterior by the displayed geometric
factors. Equivalently, eliminating \(y\) with the cosine formula defines
a causal, locally Lipschitz integral equation on exterior position and
velocity histories. To initialize from the free asymptote, on a far
incoming tail take \(|x|\ge |s|/2\) and \(|x'|\le2\). The cosine formula
gives \(|y_{\ell\nu}|=O(|s|^{-\ell-1})\) uniformly in \(\nu\) for each
\(\ell\). In the norm used for \(D\), the difference of the forces for
two such histories near the free line is bounded by a constant times
\(|s|^{-5}D(s)\), using the same first-derivative and Hessian estimates
and summing the exterior geometric factors. The twice-integrated map is
a contraction sufficiently far in the past. Local continuation applies
while the orbit stays exterior.

Write \(I_j=\int_{\mathbb R}g^{-j}ds\). Here \(I_2=\pi\), \(I_3=2\),
\(I_4=\pi/2\), \(I_5=4/3\), \(I_6=3\pi/8\) and \(I_8=5\pi/16\). The
integrating factor bound for \(K\) is \(\exp(q k_2 I_6)\). An even
nonnegative function integrated against the running integral from
\(-\infty\) of another even function gives half the product of their
full integrals. Apply this to the bounds \(g^{-2}\) or \(g^{-3}\) and
\(A\), even when the orbit itself is asymmetric. On every interval where
\(D\le0.028\) this gives \[
 \begin{aligned}
 \int gL\,ds&\le q B_{\rm lag},\qquad

\subsubsection{Action discrepancy for an elastic central
branch}\label{action-discrepancy-for-an-elastic-central-branch}

Return here to the elastic \(0.55\)--\(0.60\) nm annulus and its
original energy and acceptance bands. For a deterministic elastic
central branch use its radial characteristic \(W_E(J)\) with the free
contribution subtracted and angular momentum \(J=p b\). Hamilton's
deflection identity gives
\(|(W_E-\widetilde W_E)'(J)|=|\chi-\widetilde\chi|\) with a common sign
and continuous winding convention. This is also the integrated
scattering function of
\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0605637}{Hendi et al., Eq. (3)}
(passage, with \(p\,db=dJ\)). Suppose an alternative branch reproduces
\(Y\le Y_{\rm hi}\) under the same preparation and detector
calibrations. The mean area in the annulus on which its deflection is
within \(\theta\) of forward, modulo a full turn, is at least
\([A-(Y_{\rm hi}+G)/f]_+\), where \(f>0\) and \([x]_+=\max(x,0)\).
Indeed, every ray from the complement in the good exposure would leave
the aperture. On the near-forward rays the angle error has magnitude at
least \(\chi_*-\theta\), with \(\chi_*=0.026\) rad. Integrating over
\(J\), using \(2\pi b\,db\le 2\pi b_2\,db\), yields \[
 \int {\rm Var}_{[pb_1,pb_2]}(W_E-\widetilde W_E)\,P(dE)
 \ \ge\
 \frac{p_{\min}(\chi_*-\theta)}{2\pi b_2}
 \left[A-\frac{Y_{\rm hi}+G}{f}\right]_+ .
\] Here \(p_{\min}=\sqrt{2\mu E_{\min}}\) and \(\operatorname{Var}\) is
total variation in angular momentum. Additive action constants cancel.
With the trial numbers \(f=1/2\), \(G=0\), \(Y_{\rm hi}=0.40\) square
ångströms this exceeds \(7\,10^{-38}\) J s. This bounds a change in the
mechanical scattering characteristic. A different classical force can
produce such a change. The inequality assigns no quantum of action to
it.

\textbf{Why the finite-radius premise matters.} The smooth Newton
potentials \(V_R(r)=-C_4/(r^2+R^2)^2\) have the same measured asymptotic
coefficient for every \(R>0\). In the preceding quadrature
\(B\le 2C_4/(ER^4)=\zeta\) at every radius. For \(\zeta<1\) all \(b>0\)
scatter with \(0\le\chi\le\pi\zeta/(1-\zeta)\), and the head-on ray
crosses the regular centre along a straight line. Choosing \(R\) large
enough puts every deflection below any fixed positive angular
resolution, uniformly on the specified energy band. This gives a
classical countermodel to inferring a resolvable deflection from static
polarizability alone. The finite-radius and encounter-time force
calibration would exclude it. An action-dependent theory that recovers
the calibrated Newton loss as its action tends to zero would then
exclude an entire neighbourhood of zero if the measured deficit exceeded
its errors. The reported attenuations alone establish neither that
calibration nor a universal physical \(\hbar\).

\subsubsection{Three work readings and the low-energy
limit}\label{three-work-readings-and-the-low-energy-limit}

\textbf{6.1 Sol Max, 2026-11-18.} For an equilibrated spherical atom and
a distinguishable negative test charge, the electrostatic binding energy
is convex in inverse distance. This allows three work readings to bound
the turn of an entire annulus without reconstructing a radial force or
truncating a multipole series. If the equilibrium potential has the
measured dipole coefficient in its asymptotic tail, the annulus can move
outward as the collision energy falls. The resulting low-energy
observable is the rate coefficient \(vQ_{\rm mt}\), where \(Q_{\rm mt}\)
weights each elastic collision by the fraction of incident longitudinal
momentum it transfers.

\textbf{Why charge penetration has a sign.} Fix a point nucleus of
charge \(+Ze\), a projectile of charge \(-e\) with \(e>0\), and
\(\kappa=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)\). A trial state \(a\) of the target has
internal energy \(H_a\), mobile electron number measure \(n_a\ge0\) of
mass \(Z\), and energy at clamped probe position \(\mathbf R\) \[
 H_a-E_0-\frac{Z\kappa}{|\mathbf R|}
       +\kappa\int\frac{dn_a(x)}{|\mathbf R-x|}.
\] Take the infimum over the same rotationally invariant family of
states at every \(\mathbf R\), with \(E_0\) the isolated minimum. Assume
this equilibrium work \(W(r)\) is finite and continuous for \(r>0\) and
tends to zero at infinity. An unpolarized spherical minimizing state
gives \(W(r)\le0\) by the shell theorem. For a sphere of radius
\(d<|\mathbf R|\) centred at \(\mathbf R\), the nuclear term has its
centre value as its average. Each repulsive electron kernel averages to
\(1/\max(d,|\mathbf R-x|)\), at most its centre value, including for
electrons inside the averaging sphere. Every trial energy at the centre
therefore dominates the average of \(W\) over that sphere. Taking the
infimum at the centre gives \(\Delta W\le0\) in distributions. In radial
coordinates this is precisely convexity of \(-W(1/u)\) in \(u>0\). Its
value at \(u=0\) is zero and it is nonnegative, so it is nondecreasing.
The sign uses a negative probe and negative mobile charges. Exchange
with the probe, magnetic forces and delayed relaxation are separate
physical assumptions. Newton's shell theorem applies to each frozen
state even when the minimum responds nonlinearly.
\href{https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Mathematical_Principles_of_Natural_Philosophy_\%281729\%29/Book_1/Section_12}{Motte
1729, Book I, Section XII, Propositions LXX--LXXI} (passages). The
variational treatment of an external potential also applies to
electronic ground-state energies. The sign argument itself requires no
quantum state law. \href{https://doi.org/10.1002/qua.560240302}{Lieb
(1983)} (abstract, variational background).

\textbf{A finite preparation.} Choose an independently fixed length
\(\ell\) and relative kinetic energy \(E=\mu v^2/2\). Write \(u=\ell/r\)
and \(w(u)=-W(\ell/u)/E\). Place the three clamped probes at
dimensionless inverse radii \(u_A,u_B,u_C\) within \(0.001\) of
\(0.65,0.75,0.80\), respectively, on separate preparations of the same
atom. Take as independent readings \[
 w(u_A)\ge0.17,\qquad w(u_B)\le0.35,\qquad
 w(u_C)-w(u_B)\le0.105 .
\] For elastic motion in \(W\), every impact radius
\(2.41\le b^2/\ell^2\le2.75\) then gives a turn between \(0.156\) and
\(2.94\) rad. The calibration requires two works relative to infinity
and one work difference. A smooth exterior potential suffices for the
ordinary Newton orbit used here.

Indeed, at a turning point \(u\) one has \(b^2/\ell^2=(1+w(u))/u^2\).
This expression is above \(2.75\) at \(u_A\) and below \(2.41\) at
\(u_B\). For any intervening root put \(t=r_{\min}/r\). The
central-orbit quadrature gives the angular advance from infinity to that
root as \[
 \Phi=\int_0^1\frac{(1-P(t))^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{1-t^2}}\,dt,\qquad
 P(t)=\frac{w(u)-w(tu)}{(1+w(u))(1-t^2)},\qquad \chi=2\Phi-\pi .
\] Convexity gives \(w(tu)\le t w(u)\). The integrand is thus at least
that of a Coulomb orbit with the same turning radius and binding energy.
Its deflection is \(2\arcsin(w(u)/(2+w(u)))>0.156\). For the upper bound
every slope up to \(u_B\) is at most \(0.105/0.048\). For \(t\le0.7\)
use \(P\le(0.35/1.35)/(1-0.7^2)\). For \(t\ge0.7\) use
\(P\le0.751(0.105/0.048)/(1.17\cdot1.7)\). In particular \(P<1\)
everywhere, so the incoming orbit reaches the root and turns there. The
respective factors \((1-P)^{-1/2}\) are below \(1.43\) and \(2.42\).
Thus \(\Phi<1.43\arcsin(0.7)+2.42(\pi/2-\arcsin(0.7))<3.04\), which
excludes a full turn back into the aperture.
\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/cond-mat/0605637}{Hendi et al., Eqs.
(1)--(4)} (central-orbit quadrature) and
\hyperref[a-three-energy-test-of-central-force-trajectories]{the Coulomb
deflection formula used above}.

For example take \(\ell=(C_*/E_*)^{1/4}\simeq0.315\) nm with the fixed
references \(C_*=1.18\,10^{-3}\) eV nm\(^4\) and \(E_*=0.120\) eV. The

\textbf{The outward annulus.} Now vary \(E\), set
\(\ell_E=(C_4/E)^{1/4}\) and assume the physical work obeys
\(r^4W(r)\to-C_4<0\). This is an asymptotic material premise. For
instance the bare interaction of a spherical neutral cloud contributes
\(o(r^{-4})\) if its third radial moment is finite. Its magnitude is
then at most \(\kappa r^{-4}\int_{|x|>r}|x|^3\,dn_0(x)\). The induced
response must still give the stated coefficient. The weak uniform-field
polarizability by itself sets no finite onset radius. The scaled work
\(w_E(u)=-W(\ell_E/u)/E\) tends to \(u^4\) uniformly on bounded \(u\)
intervals. All three preceding work inequalities eventually hold. For a
stronger momentum bound take turning radii with \(m=u^4\in[1/4,0.64]\).
In this limit the quadrature becomes \[
 b^2/\ell_E^2=m^{-1/2}+m^{1/2},\qquad
 \chi(m)=2\sqrt{1+m}\int_0^{\pi/2}(1-m\sin^2 z)^{-1/2}\,dz-\pi .
\] The passage to this formula is uniform on the stated annulus. Away
from \(t=1\) it follows from uniform work convergence. Near \(t=1\) the
secants of the convex \(w_E\) on either side bound its slopes, which
therefore converge uniformly to \(4u^3\) on compact positive \(u\)
intervals. Thus \(P\) stays uniformly below one there (its limiting
maximum is \(2m/(1+m)\)). The integrable majorant is a constant times
\((1-t^2)^{-1/2}\). The turning points are accessible from infinity. The
inverse-fourth-power scaling is classical Langevin physics. The orbits
used here stay outside the capture region.
\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/0808.3620}{Grier et al., p.~1} (capture
calculation, with twice the present convention for \(C_4\)).

At \(m=1/4\) Jensen's inequality gives a turn at least
\(\pi(\sqrt{1.25/(1-0.25/2)}-1)>0.61\). At \(m=0.64\) the chord bound
for the convex integrand as a function of \(\sin^2 z\) gives an upper
angle \(\pi(\sqrt{1.64}(1+1/\sqrt{1-0.64})/2-1)<2.23\). The angle
increases with \(m\) in this formula, and the annulus area divided by
\(\ell_E^2\) tends to \(0.45\pi\). For elastic scattering define
\(Q_{\rm mt}(E)=2\pi\int_0^\infty(1-\cos\chi)\,b\,db\). Then \[
 \liminf_{E\downarrow0}vQ_{\rm mt}(E)
 \ \ge\ 0.45\pi(1-\cos 0.61)\sqrt{2C_4/\mu}
 \ >\ 0.25\sqrt{2C_4/\mu}.
\] Only the outgoing annulus is needed in the integral. If a ray is
captured, counting its full incident longitudinal momentum gives an
additional nonnegative contribution. The last rate is about
\(5.1\,10^{-15}\) m\(^3\)/s for the nominal argon coefficient. Meanwhile
the incoming angular momenta are proportional to
\(\sqrt{2\mu}\,C_4^{1/4}E^{1/4}\) and tend to zero. These two limits
refer to the same rays. Inserting additional mathematical times changes
neither.

The quiet reciprocal oscillator model above also reaches this threshold
along its \textbf{freely moving} paths. It needs no lower bound on mode
frequencies. In the earlier orbit variables compare with the \emph{pure}
static dipole orbit \(X\), taking \(s=0\) at its turn. Here \(q<0.24\)
and \((|X|^2/2)''=1-q/|X|^4\ge1-0.64\). Thus \(|X|\ge0.6g(s)\) and
\(|X'|\le\sqrt{1.64}\). While \(D\le0.1\) the previous lag estimate
applies with \(a=0.5\) and \(V=1.4\). Put \(z=(r_1/(ba))^2\), where
\(U=\sum_{\ell\ge2}w_\ell\) at the fixed \(r_1\) is finite. As
\(E\downarrow0\), \(z\to0\) and the static higher forces are bounded by
\(3q a^{-5}Uz\,g^{-5}\). Use the slow-mode bound for every higher degree
and for dipole frequencies up to a fixed \(\nu_c>0\). The squared
first-derivative factor for degree \(\ell\ge2\) is at most
\((\ell+1)^2z^{\ell-1}/4\le3z\) once \(z\le0.36\). Thus in the displayed
\(B_{\rm lag}\) bound take \(S=P_1((0,\nu_c])+3Uz\) and

\(2.3\) and \(12\) apply for sufficiently small \(Uz,z\). Adding the
static higher-force integral gives \[
 D\le q(B_{\rm lag}+3a^{-5}Uz I_4)\exp(12q a^{-6}I_4).
\] First choose \(\nu_c\) so that the slow dipole weight is arbitrarily
small, then lower \(E\) so that \(z\) and \(\Lambda\) are small. The
bound closes \(D<0.1\) and tends to zero uniformly on the annulus. The
outgoing velocities converge to the elastic reference velocities. The
annular rate bound persists for the angular moment, and for longitudinal
momentum transfer when other events have nonnegative mean loss. This
two-limit proof supplies no numerical crossover energy for real argon.

\textbf{Can finite data locate the onset?} A spherical shell of negative
charge \(-Ze\) and radius \(a\) can move by a vector of length at most
\(d<a\) relative to its fixed positive nucleus, at elastic cost
\(ks^2/2\) for displacement \(s\). At \(r>a+d\) its minimizing
displacement points away from the probe. Its work is exactly \[
 W_d(r)=\min_{0\le s\le d}\left\{\tfrac12ks^2+
 Z\kappa[(r+s)^{-1}-r^{-1}]\right\}.
\] Every \(d>0\) gives the same weak uniform-field polarizability
\((Ze)^2/k\) and the same \(C_4=(Z\kappa)^2/(2k)\) since the minimizing
\(s\) at large \(r\) is asymptotic to \(Z\kappa/(kr^2)\). Yet on any
fixed exterior radius band \(0\le W'_d(r)\le2Z\kappa d/r^3\). A
sufficiently small \(d\) makes the force arbitrarily weak on that band.
The onset of the threshold prediction is not uniform over targets with
the same measured polarizability. A support mass and an elastic
displacement stop give a classical mechanical realization.

The clocked observable for a velocity-selected beam is the loss of mean
longitudinal momentum per unit target column, divided by incident
momentum, with the angular acceptance and missing output included in its
errors. It yields \(Q_{\rm mt}\) in elastic single-atom scattering. To
approach the threshold one must keep the target spacing and cell
dimensions large compared with \(\ell_E\), use enough flight time to
resolve the relative speed, and control the target velocity spread. At
fixed nonzero target temperature a beam slowed to rest in the laboratory
samples a distribution of relative energies. Milloy et al.~give
\(Q_{\rm mt}=3.88\) square ångströms at \(0.014\) eV, fitting drift and
diffusion measurements with quantum phase shifts below \(0.32\) eV. A
finite scattering length in that fit gives \(vQ_{\rm mt}\to0\).
Inferring an action scale from this extrapolation would import the
dynamics being tested.
\href{https://www.publish.csiro.au/ph/pdf/PH770061}{Milloy et al., §4
and Tables 1--2} (method and data passages) and
\href{https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.125.1300}{O'Malley, Rosenberg and
Spruch (1962)} (abstract, polarization threshold theory).

\textbf{A finite current readout.} Prepare two dilute electron samples
and neutral targets with independently checked Maxwell velocities at the
same temperature \(T\). Give the electrons opposite finite velocity
increments \(\pm U\hat z\), keeping the target preparation the same.
Turn off the drive and measure both mean longitudinal velocities over
the same flight interval \(\Delta t\). Let \(D_n(t)\) be half their
difference at target number density \(n\), so \(D_n(0)=U\). After
empty-cell subtraction take the dilute slope \[
 {\cal R}(T,U)=-\frac{1}{U\Delta t}
 \left.\frac{d}{dn}\big[D_n(\Delta t)-D_n(0)\big]\right|_{n=0}.
\] Use completed, independent binary encounters in a homogeneous sample
and count the entire velocity distribution. In binary kinetics an
elastic encounter of relative velocity \(\mathbf g\) changes the
electron velocity by \((\mu/m_e)(\mathbf g'-\mathbf g)\). The angular
integral of that change per unit time and density is
\(-(\mu/m_e)\mathbf g\,gQ_{\rm mt}\). At first order in density the
field-free incident distributions stay fixed in the collision integral
over the gate. The drifted-Maxwell momentum-transfer law and its
constant rate for an exact inverse-speed cross section are established.
\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/0804.2856}{Pinto and Galli, Eqs. (2)--(8)
and case (b) after Eq. (16)} (binary elastic force, velocity average and
polarization law).

Let \(f_T(\mathbf g)\) be the centered, normalized Maxwell density of
the relative velocity with component variance \(k_BT/\mu\). The two
prepared currents measure the positive average \[
 \begin{aligned}
 {\cal R}(T,U)&=\frac{\mu}{m_e}\int gQ_{\rm mt}(\mu g^2/2)\,dP_{T,U},\\
 dP_{T,U}&=\frac{g_z}{2U}
 [f_T(\mathbf g-U\hat z)-f_T(\mathbf g+U\hat z)]\,d^3g .
 \end{aligned}
\] The bracket has the sign of \(g_z\) and the two means differ by
\(2U\), so the weight is nonnegative and integrates to one at finite
boost. For any independently calibrated energy band on which
\(vQ_{\rm mt}\ge K\), the raw readout therefore obeys
\(\mathcal R\ge(\mu/m_e)K P_{T,U}({\rm band})\). For
\(U=\lambda\sqrt{k_BT/\mu}\) with fixed \(\lambda>0\) the weight
concentrates at zero speed as \(T\) falls. The same positive lower rate
consequently survives the thermal average with factor \(\mu/m_e\). A
bounded low-energy cross section instead gives a vanishing rate if the
high-energy contribution vanishes on each fixed positive-energy tail, as
it does for polynomially bounded cross sections with these Maxwell
preparations.

For scale, a \(1\) ns electric pulse with time integral
\(m_eU/e\simeq5.7\,10^{-8}\) V s/m supplies \(U=10^4\) m/s. In a \(10\)
mm sensing region at \(90\) K, use identical \(50\) ps current gates
centred \(1\) ns apart after switch-off. At first order in density the
current is affine in field-free time, so averaging in these equal gates
preserves its difference. The pulse, actual relative speed distribution,
field uniformity, drift during the gates, detector response and losses
at the boundaries need calibration. At each energy the gate must
encompass the binary encounter and the dilute slope must resolve single
collisions. Along a threshold sequence the encounter duration grows as
\(\ell_E/v\) and the density must allow \(n\ell_E^3\ll1\). A fixed clock
resolution permits longer gates. Robertson's electron drift velocities
at \(89.6\) and \(293\) K are steady-field measurements. The
opposite-boost, field-free decay is a different measurement and would
test the lower bound without an inversion for phase shifts.
\href{https://www.publish.csiro.au/ph/pdf/PH770039}{Robertson, Table 1
and §3} (instrument and drift data passages).

pp.~53, 67--82, 95} (energy calibration and averaging passages).

One sufficient overlap test uses a velocity moment. The weight
\(P_{T,U}\) has mean relative energy \(\mu U^2/2+5k_BT/2\), by the third
moments of the shifted Maxwell distributions. If the certified band
extends from zero to \(E_c\), its weight is at least
\([1-(\mu U^2/2+5k_BT/2)/E_c]_+\). For the waveguide use the
initial-signal-weighted mean of \(U(x)^2\). For the separately specified

calibration through \(27\) meV would cover more than \(26\%\). Those
settings cannot be substituted for the 1974 pulse.

The order of the energy and action limits matters. For a supplied
reduced action \(h\), the exterior Schrödinger equation in units of
\(\ell_E\) and \(E\) has kinetic coefficient
\([h/(\sqrt{2\mu E}\,\ell_E)]^2\). With
\(J_*=\sqrt{2\mu E_*}\,\ell_{E_*}\) the schedule \(E/E_*=(h/J_*)^4\)
keeps this coefficient equal to one while \(h\) and \(E\) tend to zero.
The core boundary condition, clocked preparation and readout still enter
