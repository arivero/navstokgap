# Newton's comparison: areas, action defects and controlled reunion

For horizontal speed $v>0$ and downward force $F>0$, Galileo's inertial line
and falling parabola enclose area $A=vF\tau^3/(6m)$, with the exact conversion

$$\boxed{(3F/v)A=\tau\Delta E,\qquad\Delta E=F^2\tau^2/(2m).}$$

The matched-endpoint chord lens is half that area and has action defect
$F^2\tau^3/(24m)=\tau\Delta E/12$. A genuine receding-centre limit preserves
this chord defect but cancels the apparent tangent-sector remainder. A
specified $F,-F,F$ force loop reunites both position and velocity and gives
an exact scalar quantum phase for every common motional preparation, once
quantum kinematics and coherent control are supplied. None of these geometric
or phase statements establishes independent positive-action necessity.

For the short-time force measurement in Lemma X, two bounded, increasing
forces can differ at release yet give the same final motion and even the
same forced-particle propagator at the first reading. A bound on how
fast the force changes restores uniform recovery with sufficient
differential position accuracy. A separate test using three position
probabilities gives a conditional lower bound on the trial action,
with an explicit allowance for a recoiling source.
For two trapped ions there is already a measured spin signal. Its
attribution to a force transmitted through the ions' motion admits a
quantitative test using the optical wavelength and the trap clock.
For a circular orbit, three direct angle readings on separately
prepared pairs give a test that tolerates finite ruler and clock
resolution. A classical impulse model reproduces the ideal mean
fluorescence of a published rotor echo.
For a single harmonically trapped ion, three published fluorescence
contrasts give $1.768$ against a classical ceiling of $1.5$ for an
additive position readout. A zero-mean probe impulse can reproduce
the three contrasts. Direct position exposures would test the same
inequality with an independent force calibration.

Four releases of a bound pair into different central forces can bound its
initial energy spread and test Newtonian motion from final speeds alone.
Two measured hydrogen fragment spectra already test how the capture
process samples the orbit. [Four central-force releases](#four-central-force-releases-measured-by-final-speeds)
and [hydrogen fragment counts](#what-two-hydrogen-fragment-spectra-already-test).
For an inverse-square attraction, a binding-energy tag and one radius
count distinguish a factor of two in trial action. The measured bare
lithium fraction after helium-6 decay tests how often another force
must supply work when the central attraction increases.

This treatment integrates the constant-force derivation and its receding-centre
correction with the controlled reunion. Written model/coordinator checks are
internal; the mechanics and phase ingredients are established and no
literature-priority claim is made.

## The source geometry and three different areas

Take $m,F,v,\tau>0$, downward $y$, and

$$q_I(t)=(vt,0),\qquad q_F(t)=(vt,Ft^2/(2m)),\quad0\le t\le\tau.$$

The inertial–fall region is closed by the vertical segment at $x=v\tau$.
Direct integration gives $A=vF\tau^3/(6m)$, sagitta
$s=F\tau^2/(2m)$ and deterministic transverse kinetic gain
$\Delta E=Fs=F^2\tau^2/(2m)$. Total mechanical energy is conserved;
the potential loses that amount. This $\Delta E$ is not a quantum energy
standard deviation.

| Region | Positive ordinary area |
| --- | --- |
| Inertial horizontal line to falling parabola | $vF\tau^3/(6m)$ |
| Matched-endpoint chord to falling parabola | $vF\tau^3/(12m)$ |
| Inertial line to endpoint chord | $vF\tau^3/(4m)$ |

The first is twice the second; the third is their sum. Kepler sectors about
a force centre are another construction. A horizontal Galilean boost to
speed $v$ removes the plotted spatial area, while a matched-endpoint action
difference remains: the common horizontal action cancels. The conversion
therefore specifies a frame with $v\ne0$.

## Lemma X and the measurement of initial force

In Book I, Lemma X, Newton changes *vi regulari* in the London 1687
edition to *Vi finita* in the Cambridge 1713 edition, specifying a force
that is constant, continually increasing or continually decreasing. In
the new Corollary 4 he infers force from initial displacement divided by
time squared, as a proportion. Both proofs still represent velocities
as ordinates, identify displacement with the area and invoke Lemma IX.
The 1687 proof of IX already constructs a similar figure. The 1713 proof
explicitly holds the length $Ae$ fixed (*manente longitudine Ae*) while
the original points approach $A$. After X, the new Corollary 5 inverts
the same proportion for time squared, and the ensuing Scholium explains
what proportionality between quantities of different kinds means.
The fixed segment belongs to the geometric construction. Its scale on a
diagram supplies no physical resolution for a moving body.
[1687, Lemmas IX–X](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/diplomatic/NATP00077#par25)
and [1713, Lemmas IX–X, corollaries and Scholium](https://www.newtonproject.ox.ac.uk/view/texts/diplomatic/NATP00082#par26)
(diplomatic transcriptions, passages).

Newton explains his decision to defer publication in the preface to the
1687 edition. Halley urged him to communicate his orbital
proof. Newton says he postponed publication to investigate more subjects
together, explicitly including the measurement of gravity and other
forces. Earlier in that preface he treats accurate construction of a
line or circle as something geometry asks of mechanics. He attributes
ordinary errors to the practitioner. These passages connect force
measurement to the delay and physical construction to the proof method.

**What the marks have to resolve.** Read positions $y$ against an
inertial ruler at the calibrated times $0,\tau/2,\tau$. Let
$D_\tau y=y(0)-2y(\tau/2)+y(\tau)$. This cancels an unknown initial
position and velocity, including a common affine ruler drift. For
$m q''=f(t)$ with continuous $f$, the normalized second divided
difference averages $f$ with weight $4\min(t,\tau-t)/\tau^2$ on the
window. This weight integrates to one and has mean time $\tau/2$.
[De Boor, §9, Eqs. (47)–(48)](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~deboor/sat/papers/2/2.pdf#page=17)
(passage, Peano kernel). Thus a single fixed force gives its initial
value in the limit. For a class with an independently calibrated bound
$|f(t)-f(0)|\le Lt$ and differential record error
$|D_\tau y-D_\tau q|\le\rho_\tau$,

$$
\left|\frac{4m}{\tau^2}D_\tau y-f(0)\right|
\le \frac{L\tau}{2}+\frac{4m\rho_\tau}{\tau^2}.
$$

Here $L$ has units force/time and $\rho_\tau$ is a length. Calibration of
the gate times or their contribution to $\rho_\tau$ is part of the
protocol. Uniform recovery follows if $m\rho_\tau/\tau^2\to0$. For a
specified force accuracy it suffices to keep the right side below that
accuracy. The mass $m$ is calibrated by inertia. To see the distinction
from Lemma IX's fixed comparison length, fix a clock interval $T>0$
and magnify the recorded difference by $(T/\tau)^2$. For constant $f$,
its signal is now $fT^2/(4m)$ and its input error bound is
$(T/\tau)^2\rho_\tau$. Magnifying the diagram supplies no improvement
in force accuracy. A transducer acting before the position error enters
would be an additional physical operation. Classical mechanics allows
arbitrarily accurate marks.
For linear three-meter measurements of a free mass under a prescribed
force, the same difference removes the initial test-body variables.
Measuring a suitable combination of the middle pointer's position and
momentum also cancels its recoil from the output, at a cost in meter
preparation. This prevents inferring an irreducible record error from
the test mass's initial spread alone.
[Braginsky et al., §II B, Eqs. (2.21)–(2.24), (2.29)–(2.33)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/gr-qc/0109003#page=7)
(passage).

**Two forces hidden before the first reading.** Fix a force unit $F_*>0$
and any interval of duration $s>0$ between release and the first
positive observation. Write $u=t/s$ and define on $[0,1]$

$$
\begin{gathered}
H(u)=u^4(1-u)^6/24,\qquad h=H^{(4)},\\
\eta=\frac{1}{2(1+\|h\|_\infty+\|h'\|_\infty)},\qquad
f_\pm(t)=F_*[1+u\pm\eta h(u)].
\end{gathered}
$$

Both forces are positive and strictly increasing. Their slopes are at
least $F_*/(2s)$, and $h(0)=1$, $h(1)=h'(1)=0$. Continue both by
$F_*(3-\exp(1-u))$ for $u\ge1$. They are continuously differentiable,
bounded by $3F_*$, and differ at release by $2\eta F_*$ independently
of $s$. Four integrations by parts give $\int_0^1u^j h(u)\,du=0$ for
$0\le j\le3$, since $H$ and its first three derivatives vanish at both
ends. The first two moments match the endpoint impulse and displacement
for every common initial state. Within the window the position difference
is $2\eta F_*s^2 H''(u)/m$ and afterwards it is zero. Thus even a
continuous position record with fixed positive error can be shared by
choosing $s$ small enough and taking the midpoint of the two records.

The pair also tests phase-sensitive readout. For the prescribed
Hamiltonians $p^2/(2m)-f_\pm(t)q$ with a common scalar-potential zero,
the [general force propagator identity](cut-measure-newton.md#general-force-histories-one-dirichlet-functional)
(Theorem G7(a), passage) reduces endpoint evolution to two kicks and
the scalar action $\langle f,G_s f\rangle/(2m)$, where $G_s$ is the
Dirichlet inverse of $-d^2/dt^2$ on $[0,s]$. The kicks agree by the
first two moments. With $b(t)=1+t/s$, the difference of the two scalar
actions is $(2\eta F_*^2/m)\int_0^s h(t/s)(G_s b)(t)\,dt$. Since
$G_s b$ is a cubic polynomial in $t$, the remaining moments make this
integral zero.
The two body propagators are identical, including their phase, for every
supplied reduced action $a>0$. This covers endpoint interference against
a common reference. A record of the drive or an intervention within
the interval is additional data. At each smaller first gate the
construction chooses a different pair. It proves failure of a uniform
force measurement over Newton's finite monotone class and preserves the
pointwise limit for each fixed force.

**A reciprocal classical source.** Give the source a finite mass $M$,
put $\mu=mM/(m+M)$, and choose a common initial separation
$r_0$ and relative speed $v>0$. Solve $\mu\ddot r_\pm=f_\pm(t)$ from
these data. Since $\dot r_\pm\ge v$, define a time-independent pair
potential along each path by $V_\pm'(r_\pm(t))=-f_\pm(t)$. It exerts
opposite forces on the two masses. At $s$ the separations and relative
speeds agree by the matched moments. Free centre-of-mass motion then
gives identical endpoint positions and momenta of **both** masses. With
equal initial potential zeros, the work identity
$V_\pm(r_0)-V_\pm(r_\pm(s))=\mu(\dot r_\pm(s)^2-v^2)/2$
also matches their terminal energies and allows a common continuation.
During the window $\dot r_\pm\le v+2F_*s/\mu$ and
$r_\pm(s)-r_0\le vs+F_*s^2/\mu$. The interaction changes over a
shrinking length. Its force gradient at the join has magnitude
$F_*/(s\dot r_\pm(s))$. Fixed masses, initial speed and bounded exchanged work
allow these potentials in classical mechanics. Their fabrication and
independent inspection would require a source model of their own.
The propagator equality above applies to the prescribed uniform fields.
This reciprocal realization establishes classical endpoint equality for
the stated initial preparation.

## A radius count beyond the Kepler turning point

**6.1 Sol Max, 2026-11-18.** Prepare a bound pair in the attractive
potential $-K/r$. Select it by the mechanical energy released when it
forms. A single count in a radial annulus separates two reduced actions
that pass the same energy cut. The separation holds at every observation
time, with arbitrary angular momentum in the accepted band. Newton's
initial displacement test enters through the calibration of $K$.

**Preparation and detector.** Let $m$ be the reduced mass and $b$ a
length fixed by a ruler. Set $B=K/(2b)$ and $\delta=B/1000$. The energy
zero is a separated pair at rest. Accept binding energies in
$[B-\delta,B+\delta]$. One operational specification is three-body
recombination. Measure the incoming kinetic energy, the departing
third body's kinetic energy and the centre-of-mass recoil of the
surviving pair. Energy conservation gives the binding energy by
subtraction, after accounting for remaining interaction energy, missing
radiation and work by the apparatus. The resolutions and those corrections
must put the *retained* pair in the stated band. Time of flight needs
the flight lengths, times and constituent masses. A spectral line
converted to energy with a trial action would change the preparation.

Calibrate the relative force by acceleration and separation in independent
shots, $K=mr^2|{\ddot{\boldsymbol r}}|$, in the regime of the test.
Hold the pair in that field until an external gate of duration
$\Delta t>0$ centred at $t_g>\Delta t/2$. Set both times by a laboratory
clock. The radius counter answers whether the observed separation lies
between $3b$ and $8b$, with radial error at most $\sigma=b/10$.
For example, fix the ruler at $b=52.95$ pm and fix the clock at
$t_g=10$ ps, $\Delta t=1$ ps, independently of the trial action.
Equivalently, its effect lies between the position projections for the
annulus contracted and expanded by $\sigma$ at each edge. This is a
specific detector calibration, including disturbance before the gate.
Use one reading per retained pair, with all tags in the denominator.
The bounds below hold throughout the gate. They permit an independent
distribution of exposure times within it. The masses, incoming and
outgoing energies, $K$, $b$, the energy and position resolutions, and the
clock settings are the dimensional inputs. Neither the binding-energy
selection nor the annulus refers to an atomic length calculated from
$\hbar$. The position resolution is a physical requirement of the proposed
experiment.

**Two actions through the same preparation.** Write $a$ for the trial
reduced action in the spinless Coulomb Hamiltonian
$H_a=-a^2\nabla^2/(2m)-K/r$. Compare $a_0=\sqrt{mKb}$ and $a_0/2$.
The established binding energies and radial laws use
$b_a=a^2/(mK)$ and $B_n=K/(2n^2 b_a)$. At $a_0$ the cut admits only
$n=1$. At $a_0/2$ it admits only $n=2$, including both angular
subspaces. The state is stationary within either band. A spherical
counter has zero interference between different angular subspaces.
[Fitzpatrick, *Hydrogen Atom*, bound energies and normalized radial

$S_1(y)=e^{-2y}(1+2y+2y^2)$. For $a_0/2$ use $x=r/b_{a_0/2}=4r/b$.
Integrating the tabulated $2s$ and $2p$ radial densities over the

$$
 S_{2s}(x)=e^{-x}(1+x+x^2/2+x^4/8),\qquad
 S_{2p}(x)=e^{-x}(1+x+x^2/2+x^3/6+x^4/24).
$$

For $x>2$ the larger tail is $S_{2s}$. Thus even a detector with an

$$
 \begin{aligned}
 a=a_0:\quad &p_{\rm ann}\ \ge S_1(3.1)-S_1(7.9)>0.05,\\

$$

The projection bounds make this comparison valid even for a
non-spherical response inside the edge strips. The smaller-action $2s$

Both energy filters must have nonzero yield. No assumption about their

**A lower bound for every trial action.** For Newtonian relative motion
the positivity of kinetic energy gives $r\le K/(B-\delta)$ at every
instant. Even the expanded annulus starts beyond this radius. The
conclusion tolerates any collision continuation conserving the admitted
energy. For quantum motion let $P$ be the energy-band projection and let
$f$ rise linearly from zero at $R_0=5b/2$ to one at $R_1=29b/10$.
Write $w=R_1-R_0=2b/5$, $D=B-K/R_0=B/5$, and $X=\|fP\|$. The
one-cutoff localization identity applied to any unit $\psi=P\psi$ gives

$$
 D\|f\psi\|^2
 \le \operatorname{Re}\langle f\psi,f(H_a+B)\psi\rangle
         +\frac{a^2}{2mw^2}
 \le \delta X^2+\frac{a^2}{2mw^2}.
$$

Here kinetic energy after multiplication by $f$ is nonnegative,
$|\nabla f|\le1/w$, and $(H_a+B)\psi$ is still in the band. These
statements apply to the usual self-adjoint Coulomb Hamiltonian in
three dimensions. Taking the supremum and using $p_{\rm ann}\le X^2$
also covers mixtures and arbitrary observation times.

$$
                 a^2\ \ge\ 2m w^2(D-\delta)\,p_{\rm low}.
$$

Use a lower confidence bound $p_{\rm low}$ on the absolute count, after

$\eta$ of a quantum state's energy probability is outside the band,
projection and renormalization can change the count by at most

$a>0.035\sqrt{mKb}$. All lengths and times stay fixed as a mathematical
mesh shrinks. The premise requiring experimental evidence is the joint
binding-energy certificate and exterior count on the same preparation.
For this lower bound alone the inner potential can even change. It
suffices that the same separated-pair energy zero and the inequality
$U+B\ge D$ hold in every admitted channel on $r\ge R_0$ and that the
local kinetic form is the one used above. The exact two-action
probabilities use the Coulomb law throughout.

## A stronger Coulomb attraction and the measured bare lithium fraction

**6.1 Sol Max, 2026-11-18.** The decay of one-electron
$^{6}{\rm He}^{+}$ increases the nuclear charge from $2e$ to $3e$.
Couratin and collaborators measured a bare $^{6}{\rm Li}^{3+}$ fraction
of $0.02339\pm0.00036$ by detecting beta particles and timing the daughter
ions. It is an actual escape count in a system with a stronger final
attraction. Their quantum calculation gives $0.02322$ for their acceptance.
The classical question is how much energy an escaping electron can
receive from the emitted beta and other forces during the change.
[Couratin et al., Eqs. (1)–(5)](https://in2p3.hal.science/in2p3-00709325v1/document#page=4)
(passages) and [apparatus, cuts and energy calibration](../docs/CouratinEtAl_Helium6_ChargeReadout.md).

Let $k=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$, $Z=2$, and $B>0$ be the initial electron
binding energy in $-Zk/r$. Write $\mu_i,\mu_f$ for the reduced electron
masses and $\boldsymbol p$ for the initial relative momentum. Work in
the parent centre-of-mass frame. Write $\boldsymbol q$ for the impulse
from the nuclear decay and $\boldsymbol V=\boldsymbol q/M_f$, where
$M_f$ is the daughter nuclear mass. If the spectator receives no
impulse at the vertex, its final relative momentum is
$\boldsymbol p-\mu_f\boldsymbol V$. This follows by subtracting its
share of the total recoil momentum. It accounts for the change of
reduced mass even on an orbit with large initial speed. For a point
charge switching instantaneously the new relative energy is

$$
 \begin{aligned}
 E_f={}&-(1+1/Z)B+\frac{|\boldsymbol V|^2}{2}(\mu_f+d^{-1})
           -\frac d2\left|\boldsymbol p+\frac{\boldsymbol V}{d}\right|^2,\\
 d={}&(1/Z-g)/\mu_i,\qquad
 g=\mu_i/\mu_f-1=\mu_i(1/M_f-1/M_i).
 \end{aligned}
$$

This follows by substituting $Zk/r=B+|\boldsymbol p|^2/(2\mu_i)$ in
the final energy. The daughter is lighter and $\mu_i/M_f<10^{-4}$, so
$0\le g<10^{-4}$. The authors give a maximum nuclear recoil of
about $1.4$ keV. Even allowing $1.5$ keV, the mass-six daughter's
ratio $\mu_f/M_f<9.3\times10^{-5}$ gives
$\mu_f|\boldsymbol V|^2/2<0.14$ eV. Its multiplier in the
energy ceiling is $1+(1+g)/(1/Z-g)<3.001$. At $B\ge54$ eV
every post-switch trajectory is more than $80$ eV below escape,
irrespective of the direction or speed at release. The quoted
experimental binding energy is $54.4$ eV. A charge-state designation
alone leaves excited parents possible.

**Finite time to uncover the daughter charge.** Allow the emitted beta
charge to occupy an outgoing spherical shell of arbitrary increasing
radius $L(t)$, initially zero, around the coasting daughter. Its
potential energy with the spectator is $k/\max(r,L)$. The reference
potential is therefore

$$
 U(r,t)=-\frac{(Z+1)k}{r}+\frac{k}{\max(r,L(t))},
 \qquad \partial_t U=-\frac{k\dot L}{L^2}\,{\bf1}_{r<L}\le0 .
$$

The sign holds also for a positive mixture of expanding shells.
In the daughter frame let $W$ be the accumulated signed work on the
relative motion by all forces in excess of this reference, including
any subsequent acceleration of the centre. At the instant of recoil
the screening still gives the old potential. For
$E_R=\mu_f V_*^2/2$, $|\boldsymbol V|\le V_*$ and initial kinetic
energy $T_i=|\boldsymbol p|^2/(2\mu_i)$, the energy available at
any later escape is bounded above by

$$
             -B+E_R+gT_i+2\sqrt{(1+g)E_R T_i}+W .
$$

This sign follows along the actual path and allows the electron to move
throughout the field change. A measured outgoing beta direction defines
an individual nonspherical force which belongs in $W$. Averaging its
source over directions before solving for a trajectory changes the
escape problem.

There is a bound even for very eccentric initial orbits whose periapsis
speed can be arbitrarily large. Assume the nuclear decay samples time
on an initial Kepler orbit uniformly, conditional on its energy and
eccentricity. Acceptance or preparation errors in this premise have to
be included when applying the bound. Take $B\ge54$ eV,
$E_R\le0.14$ eV and $W\le20$ eV. If $T_i/B\le37$, the sum of the
two kinetic terms divided by $B$ is at most
$0.0037+2\sqrt{1.0001\times37\times0.14/54}<0.624$.
Escape requires at least $1-20.14/54>0.627$.
Hence $T_i>37B$. With $A=Zk/(2B)$ this requires
$r/A=2B/(B+T_i)<1/19$. For eccentric
anomaly $u\in[-\pi,\pi]$ the orbit has $x=r/A=1-e\cos u$ and time
measure $x\,du/(2\pi)$. For $0<s<1$, $x\le s$ implies
$|u|\le\arccos(1-s)$. On that interval the accepted time density
is at most $s/(2\pi)$. Thus

$$
 \Pr(r/A\le s)\le \frac{s}{\pi}\arccos(1-s)
       \le \frac{s}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2s}{1-s/2}} .
$$

The last inequality uses $\arcsin y\le y/\sqrt{1-y^2}$.
The bound is uniform in orbital orientation, eccentricity and recoil
direction. [Savransky, Cady and Kasdin, §3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1012.1526)
(Kepler time measure). At $s=1/19$ the bound is at most
$2/(19\pi\sqrt{37})<0.0056$.
Even taking the reported fraction two quoted standard errors below
its centre, over $0.017$ of the sample would require more than
$20$ eV of additional work. Subtract from this margin any upper
bounds on weakly bound parents, phase selection and false bare-ion
tags beyond the reported corrections.

The experimental inputs here are electronic binding energy, nuclear
recoil energy and the daughter and electron masses; the laboratory
readout uses beta energy, ion flight distance and time, and electrode
voltages. The Coulomb coefficient cancels from the final fraction.

## Four central-force releases measured by final speeds

**6.1 Sol Max, 2026-11-18.** The same four sets of outgoing speeds
can bound the energy spread of a bound pair and test its equation of
motion. The test uses every release. It requires neither a selected
angular momentum nor a measurement of the initial energy level.

Let $R=|\mathbf r|$ be the relative separation and $\mu$ the reduced
mass. Prepare the same ensemble in the central well $-Q(R)$ on each
trial. Choose $Q$ smooth, bounded, nonnegative and nonincreasing, and
zero outside a calibrated radius $r_b$. At a time chosen independently
of the motion, switch to $(j-1)Q(R)$, with $j=1,2,3,4$ chosen
independently of the preparation. Thus the first setting releases the
pair freely and the other three repel the particles. Outside the field
record their relative kinetic energy $K_j=\mu v_{\rm rel}^2/2$ by
time of flight. Include late, undetected and saturated events in the
moment-error budget. The potential zero is common at infinity.
Calibrate $L=\sup Q-\inf Q$ in energy units and $F_*=\sup|Q'|$ in
force units. In Lemma X's prescription, compare relative accelerations
from rest at the same radius in the two fields. An envelope between
sampled radii and an integration in radius give the force and work
bounds. The [finite-time qualifications](#lemma-x-and-the-measurement-of-initial-force)
apply to this calibration.

Write $v_j$ for the variance and $t_j$ for the **third central moment**
of the energy reading in setting $j$. Keep energies in mechanical
units for every setting. Form

$$
 S=3v_1-3v_2+v_3,\qquad
 D=\tfrac12(3t_1-8t_2+7t_3-2t_4).
$$

For any ensemble of Newtonian pairs subject to the stated force
switch and an instantaneous release,

$$
                  |D|\le \tfrac32 L^2\sqrt S.                   \tag{F}
$$

This allows arbitrary initial correlations between separation, radial
speed and angular momentum. It also allows additive energy-readout
noise independent of the atom with a common distribution of finite
third absolute moment. A constant energy offset may differ by setting.

Indeed let $E$ be the initial mechanical energy and $q=Q(R)$ at the
switch. The outgoing energy on this same trajectory is $E+jq$, since
the switch changes the potential and preserves the velocity. Its
variance is a quadratic in $j$ with constant term $\operatorname{Var}E$.
The combination $S$ gives this constant plus the common noise variance.
The third central moment is a cubic in $j$. The weights defining $D$
extract its quadratic coefficient, canceling the common noise skew as
well. This coefficient is
$3\operatorname{Cov}(E,(q-\langle q\rangle)^2)$.
The squared centred quantity is between zero and $L^2$, so its
standard deviation is at most $L^2/2$. This proves (F). Inserting
times anywhere in the incoming or outgoing orbit preserves the
work identity. A classical trajectory that waits forever at an
unstable equilibrium requires an allowance if the detector times out.
[Bhatia and Davis, range bound for variance](https://doi.org/10.1080/00029890.2000.12005203)
(theorem).

**The action measured by an excess.** In canonical relative motion
write $H=\mathbf p^2/(2\mu)-Q(R)$ with trial reduced action $a$.
Use the same density operator for all four switches. Assume finite
third absolute energy moments, the operator products below well defined
(for example on a common smooth core with the indicated moments),
and collection of the full outgoing channel. The spectral law of
$H+jQ$ then gives the asymptotic kinetic-energy law by the
intertwining of the outgoing wave operator. Smooth short-range
potentials and states in the scattering subspace suffice.
[Enss, short-range scattering](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01940771)
(theorem).

Let $A=H-\langle H\rangle$ and $B=Q-\langle Q\rangle$. The same
variance interpolation gives $S=\langle A^2\rangle$ plus any common
readout variance. The quadratic coefficient in
$\langle(A+jB)^3\rangle$ is

$$
 D=3\,\operatorname{Re}\langle A B^2\rangle
       +\tfrac12\langle[B,[A,B]]\rangle
   =3\,\operatorname{Re}\langle A B^2\rangle
       +\frac{a^2}{2\mu}\langle |Q'(R)|^2\rangle .
$$

The local double commutator is the standard energy-weighted sum
rule. The appearance of a double commutator in the third moment
of sudden quantum work is also established. Here the coefficient
comes from four *terminal* kinetic-energy laws and the variance
of the bound preparation is tested by those same flights.
[Hinohara, Eqs. (16)–(18)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.11005)
and [Ma, Mitchell and Sela, sudden-quench formula on p. 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.01601)
(passages). Since $\langle A\rangle=0$, Cauchy–Schwarz gives the same
bound $\tfrac32L^2\sqrt S$ on the first term even when the state has
energy coherences. Consequently, if simultaneous moment bounds give
$S\le S_{\rm hi}$, $D\ge D_{\rm lo}$ with $S_{\rm hi}\ge0$, and the bracket below is positive, the bound is

$$
 a\ \ge\
 \frac{\sqrt{2\mu\,[D_{\rm lo}-\tfrac32 L^2\sqrt{S_{\rm hi}}]}}{F_*}.
                                                               \tag{A}
$$

For an initial bound eigenstate with exact readout, $S=0$ and
$D=a^2\langle |Q'|^2\rangle/(2\mu)$. A nonconstant smooth well
and a bound state with weight on its slope give a strict excess in
this model. Establishing it for physical pairs requires the four
measured distributions.

**Measuring the force factor in a Coulomb release.** With a fifth
strength and finite fourth moments the measured flights can also

$$
 V_q=(v_1-2v_2+v_3)/2,\qquad
 M_c=(m_5-4m_4+6m_3-4m_2+m_1)/24.
$$

The highest coefficients of the two polynomials are
$V_q=\langle B^2\rangle$ and $M_c=\langle B^4\rangle$ in both theories.
An independent common detector error adds only lower powers of $j$.
Thus the nuisance term in $D$ is bounded by
$3\sqrt{S(M_c-V_q^2)}$, using only terminal energy readings.
This works for any reference $H=\mathbf p^2/(2\mu)+V_b(R)$ and any
common input state if all five exit surfaces are $V_b+jQ$. Preparing
the pair on $V_b$ is unnecessary.

For a repulsive Coulomb increment $Q(R)=\kappa/R$, where $\kappa$ has
units of energy times length, let $M_r$ be the same fourth difference
divided by 24 using **raw** fourth moments. Use a common energy zero or
calibrate offsets before taking these raw moments. Then $M_r=\langle Q^4\rangle$ and $|Q'|^2=Q^4/\kappa^2$. The canonical energy laws give

$$
 \left|D-\frac{a^2 M_r}{2\mu\kappa^2}\right|
           \le 3\sqrt{S(M_c-V_q^2)}.
$$

This compares trial actions in mechanical units even when the initial
radial distribution is unknown. It requires common preparation and
response, the indicated finite moments and commutator domains at small
$R$, and an escaping channel whose true energy is measured.
Use Coulomb scattering asymptotics and bound residual potential energy

compact well does not cover a $1/R$ tail. [Dollard, Coulomb scattering](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.1704171)
(abstract). Independent mass comparison and classical charge–force

**Finite readings.** Fix the free-flight ruler, clock interval $\tau$,

integrating the change in strength times the potential along the
moving orbit. Calibrating (A) also requires bounds on any finite-ramp
change of the quantum energy moments or a justified sudden limit.

centred powers. For unbounded or missing records bound the respective

force profiles, field radius, release and readout distances, timing and
resolution, and any speed, energy or moment limits used for these

It follows that $S=0$ and $D=3b_0^3/32>0$ at zero action.
The field now supplies additional work that a mean-force calibration
would miss. On an independently prepared narrow-radius, narrow-speed
calibration flight this model predicts an extra rms work of
$j\sqrt3(32D/3)^{1/3}/4$. A bound on such work over the occupied
radii and speeds belongs in the moment allowances above.

## What two hydrogen fragment spectra already test

Schmidt et al. measured the two neutral hydrogen fragments and the
helium recoil after an electron transferred to a hydrogen molecular
ion. Two of their plots give **counts against final kinetic energy
release** $K$ for two recoil-selected bands, labelled $\nu=2,3$.
A peak in the ratio of these two spectra tests a classical radial
orbit without converting any fragment energy to a bond length.
[Schmidt et al., Fig. 4(d,e)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1202.3899#page=5)
(experimental points and caption).

Let the pair undergo stationary bound vibrations in an incoming
potential $V(R)$ at common angular momentum $J$. Write
$U(R)=V(R)+J^2/(2\mu R^2)$. Sample each orbit uniformly in elapsed
time. On a common traversed interval where $E>U(R)$, the usual
time-spent density is proportional to $[E-U(R)]^{-1/2}$. The
normalization includes the period and source flux. As an experimental
control, a collision time randomized uniformly over a duration $T$
leaves at most $P/(4T)$ total-variation error from a uniform phase
on an orbit of period $P$. Indeed the remainder $r=T\bmod P$
contributes $r(P-r)/(PT)$. A steady source alone need not implement
this control. [Cambridge, WKB and the classical time-spent density](https://www.tcm.phy.cam.ac.uk/~bds10/aqp/handout_approx.pdf)
(passage).

Suppose electron attachment preserves the nuclear relative momentum
and switches to the same outgoing potential $V_f(R)$, with its zero
at infinite separation. Then $Y=K-E=V_f(R)-V(R)$, up to the common
choice of the initial energy zero. Full energy balance identifies
this difference with the change of translational energy with reversed
sign, up to the same fixed channel offset. It is accessible from
fragment and recoil momenta. On the interval under test assume the
map from $R$ to $Y$ is one-to-one and monotone, all radii contributing to the windows
are interior to that branch in the same traversed well for both bands, and $U$ falls
then rises or is monotone there. Abrupt turning endpoints inside a
window would invalidate the time-spent comparison. Allow an arbitrary
common efficiency $w(R)$ for transfer and collection, and an arbitrary
normalization for each incoming band.

For two sharp energies $E_l<E_h$, the ratio of the densities in $Y$ is

$$
                 C\sqrt{\frac{E_h-U(R)}{E_l-U(R)}} .
$$

Both $w$ and the radius-to-energy Jacobian cancel. The square root
increases with $U$. The density ratio therefore has no interior peak
above both sides. The same conclusion holds for disjoint ordered
energy bands if the sorting uses the actual $K-E$ and all admitted
energies traverse the windows. For each band integrate
$(E-U)^{-1/2}$ with its positive source and inverse-period weight.
The logarithmic derivative with respect to $U$ is half a weighted
average of $(E-U)^{-1}$. Every such value in the lower band exceeds
every one in the upper band. For three ordered disjoint windows
$A,B,C$, let $N_{ij}$ denote their counts and $q_j=N_{lj}/N_{hj}$.
A bin ratio is a high-band weighted average of the density ratio.
Every middle point has ratio at most the maximum of the ratios at
any pair of flanking points. Hence $q_B\le\max(q_A,q_C)$ at finite
bin width whenever the denominators are positive.

**The plotted counts.** In units of eV use $K$ for the lower row and
$K-\Delta$ for the higher, with nominal band separation
$0.20\le\Delta\le0.30$ eV. Fig. 1(a) places the two band centres
about 0.24 eV apart. Reading the blue markers at their plotted 0.10 eV
spacing as bin counts gives the conservative bounds below. In the
upper row use only whole bins inside both extreme translates for
a lower bound, or all bins in their union for an upper bound.

| Window in the common coordinate (eV) | Lower-band counts | Higher-band counts used |
|---|---:|---:|
| $A=[0.5,0.8)$ | $<320$ | $>500$ in $[0.8,1.0)$ |
| $B=[1.0,1.3)$ | $>1040$ | $<270$ in $[1.2,1.6)$ |
| $C=[1.6,1.9)$ | $<290$ | $>310$ in $[1.9,2.1)$ |

Thus $q_B>3.85$ whereas $q_A<0.64$ and $q_C<0.94$. The ratio in
the middle exceeds the larger flanking ratio by a factor greater
than four. These are graphical counts with a range for the *centre
offset*. Eventwise band migration and statistical uncertainty still
need to be included. For example, if the only error were removal of
counts from the common-response distributions, over 575 additional
counts across the two rows would be needed to restore the inequality.
For side $s=A$ or $C$, restoring it requires
$(N_{hB}+m_{hB})(N_{ls}+m_{ls})\ge N_{lB}N_{hs}$.
The arithmetic–geometric mean bound gives at least
$2\sqrt{1040\cdot310}-270-290>575$ missed counts for $C$, and a
larger bound for $A$. It applies to these count units and to pure
losses. [Original plot, bins and recoil selection](../docs/SchmidtEtAl_HydrogenFragmentEnergies_2012.md)
(figure and apparatus passages).

There is also a test if the force shape or the map to fragment energy
is unknown. For sharp energies and common $J$, allow any common positive
response from radius to the shifted energy window, including blur. On
radii traversed by both orbits their incoming density ratio lies between
$C$ and $C\sqrt{1+\Delta/T_{\min}}$ if the lower orbit has radial kinetic
energy at least $T_{\min}$ there. The ratio is zero on the high orbit
outside the lower orbit's range. Take $T_{\min}$ only on the shared
radii contributing to $B$. Let $\eta_s$ be the fraction of high-band
counts in a side window $s$ from outside the lower orbit's range.
Positivity and integration of the response give

$$
 \frac{q_B}{q_s}\le
 \frac{\sqrt{1+\Delta/T_{\min}}}{1-\eta_s}.
$$

This argument needs neither a monotone switch map nor a one-well shape.
If the lower orbit covers the side windows, the observed factor greater
than four requires radial kinetic energy below $0.30/15=0.020$ eV
somewhere contributing to $B$. Alternatively, a measured lower speed
bound giving $T_{\min}\ge0.050$ eV would require more than
$1-\sqrt7/4>0.33$ of the high counts in each side window to come from
radii inaccessible to the lower orbit. The random phase, common
angular momentum and common response remain physical premises.

**What could make the peak classically?** If the two bands have
different radial transfer probabilities, the ratio acquires a
multiplier $w_l(R)/w_h(R)$. Its supremum divided by its infimum
on the three windows must exceed four to fit the nominal counts
under the other hypotheses. A common change with radius, however
large, cancels. In fact a velocity-sensitive classical capture law
can fit arbitrary bounded nonnegative binned spectra on a compact
interior interval. Choose a smooth single well, two regular energies
and a monotone switch function $Y(R)$ there. For row $i$ with desired
density $f_i$, take its capture probability proportional to
$f_i(Y(R))|Y'(R)|\sqrt{E_i-U(R)}$. A separate constant can keep each
probability below one because the source intensities are free. One
law of radius and speed realizes both prescriptions on the disjoint
energy surfaces and can be extended between them. Events outside
the recorded channel then carry the missing flux.

The experiment reports a radial variation of transfer probability
deduced from comparison with calculated wave functions. It leaves
the relative sensitivities of the two energy bands without an
independent calibration. The rotational state was unresolved.
The inferred radii depend on the treatment of the nuclei's initial
kinetic energy. Thus the raw spectra identify a size of effect
that a classical capture model must accommodate. A mechanical test
with the four externally chosen force releases above would replace
this selective reaction and both energy tags by a single preparation.
The measured molecular spectra supply one outgoing force surface.

## A three-time test with a calibrated position resolution

A candidate physical premise can be stated entirely in observed
frequencies. Prepare the same ensemble of bodies and independently choose
one final reading per run at $t_j=0,\tau/2,\tau$, with no earlier
measurement. For a spatially uniform calibrated acceleration $g$,
subtract $g(t_j-\tau/2)^2/2$ from each position. Write $p_j$ for the
probability that this corrected position, after the calibrated blur
below, falls in the same bounded interval $I$ of the ruler. Count all
preselected runs, with missing outcomes included in the error bounds.
The proposed observation is a positive excess

$$V=p_0+p_2-p_1-1>0.$$

The corresponding classical bound $V\le0$ is the particle reentry inequality of
[Paterek and Goussev, Eqs. (2) and (4)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.10155#page=2)
(passage), in the freely falling coordinates of the parabola. A classical
trajectory is a straight line in these coordinates. Membership of both
endpoints in $I$ implies membership of the midpoint, for every initial
place and velocity and hence for every positive distribution over them.
This also allows arbitrary extra preparation labels when the choice of
gate is independent and the calibrated detector reads the body's position.

**How a violation would exclude small actions.** Fix a ruler width
$\ell>0$ and a calibration time $T_c>0$ independently of the gate spacing.
Set $w=\ell/T_c$. Require that the total position response at each gate
be a centred Gaussian of variance $\sigma_j^2=\ell^2+w^2(t_j-\tau/2)^2$.
This is a specification for the calibrated detector response including
any blur deliberately applied to its records. It makes the three noise
marginals exactly those of adding one random affine function
$X+U(t-\tau/2)$ with independent centred Gaussian offsets of standard
deviations $\ell,w$. Thus the blurred records still obey $V\le0$ for
every classical ensemble. Measurements on different runs require no
joint draw of these offsets.

Compare also every canonical Schrödinger model with trial reduced
action $a>0$, the same mass $m$, uniform force $mg$, and Born position
readout. Its Wigner distribution at the middle time propagates by the
exact affine Newtonian flow, so blurring the three marginals is equivalent
to convolving that distribution at the middle time with a Gaussian of
covariance $\operatorname{diag}(\ell^2,m^2w^2)$ in position and momentum.
The Gaussian smoothing theorem gives a positive probability density for
every state when $\ell m w\ge a/2$. The classical interval argument then
applies to that density.
[Quadratic transport, Theorem A(b)](principia-fifth-postulate.md)
(passage) and [Cartwright, Gaussian smoothing](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-4371(76)90145-X)
(abstract). If simultaneous error bounds $e_j$ cover the three reported
probabilities at the stated joint confidence level, then

$$
\widehat p_0+\widehat p_2-\widehat p_1-1>e_0+e_1+e_2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
a>2m\ell w=\frac{2m\ell^2}{T_c}
$$

for every such candidate model. The observation also excludes the
classical zero member under the stated trajectory and readout laws.
The bound uses a mass, a length and a time supplied without quantum
calibration. The other dimensional inputs are the gate times, the
interval endpoints and the acceleration. For a spatially uniform force
with acceleration miscalibration bounded by $\delta g$, the residual
shift at gate $j$ is at most $\delta g(t_j-\tau/2)^2/2$ relative to
the middle-time tangent. A shift $d$ changes the blurred interval
probability by at most $|d|/(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_j)$, from the derivative
of the Gaussian interval probability. Other errors in the force law,
clock, detector or gate selection need their own probability bounds.

**Allowing an unobserved source to recoil.** Suppose the source remains
coupled throughout the gate. In a canonical model, add a source
Hamiltonian and a bounded self-adjoint
potential $W_t(Q)$ acting on the source at each body coordinate.
Assume the source alone has a unitary propagator, the perturbation is
norm-continuous in time, and a force calibration supplies
$\sup_q\|W_t(q+d)-W_t(q)\|\le m\delta g|d|$ for every $d,t$.
This includes bounded reciprocal pair potentials $W(q-R)$ with the
stated slope. For any joint state at the middle time $t_m=\tau/2$,
the error in gate probability caused by dropping this coupling is at most

$$b_j=\frac{\delta g(t_j-t_m)^2}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_j}.$$

Indeed, under the reference uniform force, the terminal mode $e^{ikQ}$
transported back to an intermediate time $r$ translates the argument
of $W_r$ by a distance of magnitude $a|k(t_j-r)|/m$. Its commutator
with $W_r$, divided by $a$, has norm at most $\delta g|k(t_j-r)|$.
The bounded-perturbation Duhamel formula in either time direction gives
a mode error at most $\delta g|k|(t_j-t_m)^2/2$.
For the Gaussian-blurred interval effect $B_j$,
$|k\widehat B_j(k)|\le2\exp(-\sigma_j^2 k^2/2)$ in the Fourier
convention with inverse factor $1/(2\pi)$. Integrating the mode error
gives $b_j$. The deterministic correction for $g$ translates the
interval and leaves this bound unchanged. Starting both evolutions
at the same middle-time joint state allows arbitrary body–source
correlations. The comparison probabilities are those of one common
reduced body state. For a classical source with $|\ddot q-g|\le\delta g$
on each unobserved path, the tangent displacement bound above gives
at most $b_j/2$ per gate even if the residual depends on that path.
Thus adding $\sum_j b_j$ to the required excess covers both classes.
The calibration has to bound the interaction on the admitted domain.
A mean acceleration measurement alone supplies no such bound.

Subdividing the mathematical propagation between these fixed gates
changes neither side of the test. Physically sending $\tau$ to zero
at fixed $\ell,T_c$ and fixed release preparation is different. All
three blurred probabilities then converge to the same $p<1$ by
continuity of the motion and readout, so $V\to p-1<0$. The positive
width and bounded $I$ give the strict inequality. Conversely, a strict
ideal reentry violation on a half-line persists for some finite interval
and sufficiently small positive blur by continuity of the three marginal
probabilities. Paterek and Goussev exhibit such states in the supplied
quantum theory. The missing physical input is a violation in corrected
gate counts with the response and errors independently calibrated.
[Brown et al., Fig. 3 and supplement IV B](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.03053#page=4)
(passages) reconstruct a trapped atom's state from quadratures by a
quantum maximum-likelihood fit. Their reported Wigner negativity does
not supply these three counts. A verified violation would constrain
the calibrated motional models above. Extending that constraint to all
preparations requires a further physical argument.

## Two action scales in the same atom photographs

**6.1 Sol Max, 2026-11-18.** A specified set of position photographs
in a central harmonic trap admits two reduced actions differing by a
factor of two. One calibrated quartic central impulse separates them
using the third moment of the same camera coordinate. A bounded
statistic of the photographs would already exclude zero action if the
specified profile were observed with the tolerances below.

Load the same source repeatedly, with no promise about its purity or
level populations. Prepare the ensemble by holding it in the trap for
an independently chosen time uniform over one period. Keep the load
flag. Impose any further hold only after preparation, switch off the
trap, allow free flight for time $T$, and read one terminal position.
A common independent jitter on the randomized hold, of fixed clock
width $\delta t$, preserves uniform phase modulo a period. In one
Cartesian component let $m$ be the inertial mass and let $\omega$ be
the angular frequency measured from trajectories. For a Hooke force,
$Z=x(T_{\rm release})+T p_x(T_{\rm release})/m$ projects
$(x,p_x/(m\omega))$ on a rotated axis with gain
$M=\sqrt{1+\omega^2T^2}$. Use the ruler coordinate $y=Z/M$. In these
coordinates the quantum commutator of the two projected axes is
$ia/(m\omega)$ for trial reduced action $a$. The same physical hold
averages over rotations for every $a$, and the same ruler and clock
give $M$ for every $a$.

Fix a ruler length $L$ independently. Write $G_L$ for a centred
Gaussian density of standard deviation $L$. The target at every
hold has $\eta=3/4$ and density

$$
 g(y)=G_L(y)(1-\eta+\eta y^2/L^2),\qquad
 g_{\rm camera}=G_b*g .
$$

Here $b>0$ is the independently calibrated Gaussian readout width in
$y$, or $Mb$ in the object plane. A camera bin records the integral of
$g_{\rm camera}$ over its edges. For the exact profile statement one
can scan the bin edges in ruler units. A fixed finite set of bin counts
has less information. A full profile at known Gaussian resolution
determines the intrinsic profile uniquely since the Gaussian Fourier
transform is nowhere zero. The bounded test below avoids this inverse.

**An explicit pair.** Put $a_*=2m\omega L^2$. At $a_*$ take the
stationary mixture with level probabilities $p_0=1/4,\ p_1=3/4$.
At $a_*/2$ take the stationary mixture with
$p_0=0,\ p_n=4n/3^{n+1}$ for $n\ge1$. These are two assignments to
one operationally specified source. They differ on a level measurement,
which the preparation does not use. Both have the same Wigner function
in physical position and momentum, hence the same law before and
after the hold, at every flight time, and after a common camera or
clock response. The oscillator level length at $a_*/2$ is $L/\sqrt2$.
The populations above are the diagonal dilation coefficients at this
changed length. The exact compatible interval for this profile is
$[a_*/2,a_*]$. These are applications of the established
Narcowich–Wigner spectrum for the zero–one mixture. [Bröcker and
Werner, §V](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.531326) (bibliographic record)
and [Frigerio et al., Appendix A.2, Eqs. (81)–(83)](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.03544v1#A1.SS2)
(passage, including the attribution to the 1995 result).

**A finite-accuracy observation.** Give each raw position $y$ the
bounded weight

$$
 f_u(y)=u^2\int_0^\infty k e^{-u^2k^2/2}\cos(ky)\,dk,\qquad |f_u|\le1 .
$$

On a Newtonian orbit of amplitude $R$ in the projected plane the
uniform hold makes the angular mean of the cosine $J_0(kR)$. Including
camera blur and writing $v^2=b^2+u^2$, the resulting weight is
$(u^2/v^2)e^{-R^2/(2v^2)}\ge0$ by the two-dimensional Gaussian
Fourier transform. Arbitrary release ensembles inherit the sign.
For a canonical state the same average samples its Wigner density
smoothed isotropically at width $v$ at the origin, with a positive
prefactor. Gaussian smoothing gives the same sign whenever
$a\le2m\omega v^2$. Both filtered projection tests and Gaussian
positivity are established. [Fresta, Borregaard and Sørensen, Eqs. (28)–(30)](https://arxiv.org/html/1508.00828v1#S3)
(passage) and [Cartwright](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-4371(76)90145-X)
(abstract).

The target has raw cosine mean
$(1-\eta L^2k^2)\exp[-(L^2+b^2)k^2/2]$. Choose the two ruler widths
$b=u=L/\sqrt8$. Integrating this mean against the specified weight gives

$$
 \langle f_u\rangle=
 \frac{u^2[v^2+(1-2\eta)L^2]}{(L^2+v^2)^2}=-\frac1{50}.
$$

Thus an upper error endpoint below zero would exclude every classical
release and all canonical $a\le a_*/4$ under the calibrated laws,
even for profiles outside the target family. Pixel centres with pitch
$\Delta y$ cost at most $\sqrt{\pi/2}\,\Delta y/(2u)$ since this
bounds the slope of the weight times the half-pitch. Each missing
launch can contribute at most one to the unnormalized sum. Uncertainty
in flight time or force and position-dependent detection need their
own error bounds. The pair's
equality also holds for finite independent flight-time exposures,
by averaging its common law at each $T$. To use the quoted $1/50$
margin with such exposures, scale by the recorded $M(T)$ and match
the response width for each $T$, or include the flight-window error.

**A clock with finitely many settings.** Choose uniformly among $N$
holds spaced by $\pi/(N\omega)$ over a half-period. The even score
has period $\pi$ in angle. Let $Q_\theta$ be the ruler quadrature and
$V_\theta=\partial_\theta Q_\theta$. Bound
$R_2^2=\langle Q_\theta^2+V_\theta^2\rangle$ from two quarter-period
separated position readings, accounting for the common blur. This
quantity is invariant under the hold. Put $c=a/(m\omega)$, or $c=0$
on classical orbits. For the score mean $S(\theta)$ at one angle,

$$
 \sup_\theta |S''(\theta)|\le H=
 \sqrt{\pi/2}\,\frac{u^2 R_2}{v^3}
 +\frac{2u^2 R_2^2}{v^4}+\frac{2u^2 c^2}{v^6}.
$$

Indeed $U=e^{ikQ_\theta}$ has derivative $ik\{V_\theta,U\}/2$,
where braces denote the anticommutator. Differentiating again uses
$V_\theta'=-Q_\theta$ and
$\{V_\theta,\{V_\theta,U\}\}=4V_\theta U V_\theta+c^2 k^2 U$.
These expectations extend as quadratic forms to states of finite
second moment. Unitarity and Cauchy–Schwarz bound $|\langle U''\rangle|$ by
$|k|R_2+k^2 R_2^2+c^2k^4/4$. Integration against
$u^2 k e^{-v^2 k^2/2}$ gives $H$. The classical estimate follows
by ordinary differentiation at $c=0$. Averaging the symmetric
Taylor remainder in each angular cell gives error at most
$H(\pi/N)^2/24$. Independent common timing jitter obeys the same
bound, by averaging shifted grids. Additional offsets of at most
$\epsilon_t$ per setting cost at most
$\sqrt{\pi/2}\,u^2 R_2\omega\epsilon_t/v^3$ by the first derivative.

For the target $R_2^2=5L^2$. If the two position readings certify this
upper bound, $H<27$ for every $a\le a_*/4$ and every classical
candidate. With $N=32$ the grid can lower the mean by less than
$0.011$. The target is stationary and still gives $-0.020$ at these
settings. This leaves more than $0.009$ for the other errors. A
different measured second-moment bound or clock spacing goes into
the displayed formula.

**A central-force measurement separating the pair.** Translate the
phase-averaged preparation by a ruler displacement $d\ne0$ along the
observed axis. This can be done by first holding the trap centred at
$d$ and then applying a pulse about the fixed origin. The preparation
translation $e^{-id P_x/a}$ shifts both states by the same physical
$d$, or by $d/L$ and $\sqrt{2}d/L$ in their respective level units. In three
dimensions use a product with the same physical transverse Wigner
densities in the two models, for instance independent oscillator
Gaussians with position width $L$ and momentum width $m\omega L$.
Apply the radial potential impulse $J(\mathbf x)=\lambda|\mathbf x|^4/4$.
Its integrated force is $-\lambda|\mathbf x|^2\mathbf x$.
Calibrate $\lambda$ from a momentum impulse at a known radius, in

$$
 k\beta\lambda r^2 x+\tfrac14 a^2k^3\beta^3\lambda x .

The first term gives the same classical impulse in both models. The

 \mu_3(a_*)-\mu_3(a_*/2)=\frac98\,\lambda d\,(T/m)^3 a_*^2 .

## Three angle readings on a central orbit

**6.1 Sol Max, 2026-11-18.** A torque-free circular orbit permits any
initial angle and speed, yet three angular distributions at equal time
steps must satisfy (1) below with phase zero. A violation would also
bound the reduced action in the canonical planar rotor, even if the
release state were unknown. One coherent aperture gives opposite signs
in the same detector for two trial actions. Rotational fractional
revivals and their use to probe quantization are established.
[Urban et al., Eq. (1)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.05763#page=2)
(passage, planar rotor spectrum) and
[Stickler et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.01778)
(abstract, proposed orientational revival measurement).

**The three exposures.** Two equal masses $m$ rotate in a plane at fixed
separation $d$. The relative inertia is $I=\mu d^2$, with $\mu=m/2$.
Calibrate $m$ by mechanical mass comparison, $d$ by imaging, and the
gate spacing $T>0$ and duration $\delta>0$ by a clock. After one common
release, choose one of three gates independently on each run. Read
the interparticle axis just once at $jT+s$, $j=0,1,2$, where $s$ is
uniform on $[0,\delta]$ for each choice. Its distribution is independent
of the release. The record retains the gate label and angle. There
are no drives between release and detection.
For an unoriented axis choose the even harmonic $k=2$. A ruler giving
uniform arc error on $[-\ell,\ell]$ at radius $d/2$ gives angular
half-width $\eta=2\ell/d$. The observed moment of order $q$ is the true
moment times $b_q=\operatorname{sinc}(q\eta)$, with
$\operatorname{sinc}(x)=\sin(x)/x$ and value one at zero. The detector
response and any angular dependence of losses require independent
calibration. Keep all accepted releases in the denominator.

Divide the measured moments by their nonzero $b_q$. Call the results
$z_0,z_1,z_2$ in time order, using harmonics $k,2k,k$ respectively.
For classical torque-free trajectories the admissible values obey

$$
 \left|e^{-i\Phi}z_2-z_1\overline{z_0}\right|\le R,\qquad
 R=\sqrt{(1-|z_0|^2)(1-|z_1|^2)},\qquad \Phi=0.                 \tag{1}
$$

Indeed $\theta_2=2\theta_1-\theta_0$ modulo $2\pi$ for every initial
angle and speed. A common random offset $s$ couples the three time
marginals without changing any of them. Cauchy–Schwarz bounds the
mean product of the centred $e^{2ik\theta_1}$ and $e^{-ik\theta_0}$
in absolute value by $R$. Any
hidden release labels and any correlations between angle and speed
are allowed.

For a canonical rotor of trial reduced action $a>0$ the same disk
(1) holds with $\Phi=ak^2T/I$. Write $A_j(q)=U_{jT}^\dagger
e^{iq\theta}U_{jT}$. On the angular mode $|n\rangle$ the free
propagator gives phase $\exp[-ian^2t/(2I)]$, and $e^{iq\theta}$
shifts $n$ to $n+q$. Consequently
$A_2(k)=e^{i\Phi}A_1(2k)A_0(k)^\dagger$. In the common release state
averaged over $s$, subtract the means of the two unitary factors
and apply operator Cauchy–Schwarz. Their variances are exactly the
two factors in $R^2$. This proof includes mixed states. In particular,
if the measured moments give $S=|z_2-z_1\overline{z_0}|-R>0$,
then $S\le |1-e^{i\Phi}|$ and

$$
                 a\ \ge\ \frac{2I}{k^2T}\arcsin(S/2).              \tag{2}
$$

Use the infimum of $S$ over simultaneous error intervals for an
experimental bound. Equations (1) and (2) hold for every subdivision
of the motion between the physical gates.

**One preparation with a finite ruler and clock.** Start the scalar
rotor in the uniform, zero-winding mode. At release transmit amplitude
$A(\theta)=\cos^4(k\theta/2)$ and count rejects. This specifies a
coherent real aperture with no angle-dependent phase. Its transmission
intensity alone fixes the success probability $35/128$ for the uniform
input. Its amplitude law is an additional preparation premise for the
predictions that follow. Repeatability suffices for the preparation
in (1). The five accepted mode amplitudes, at
$n/k=-2,-1,0,1,2$, are $(1,4,6,4,1)/\sqrt{70}$.
Fix $\eta=1/(8k)$ and $\delta=T/(100\pi)$ before comparing models.
Use this same mask, ruler and clock under $a\mapsto 2a$.

Set $a_*=\pi I/(k^2T)$. At the nominal gates and $a=a_*$ the moments
are $(4/5,2/5,-4/5)$. The adjacent coefficient products sum to
$56/70$ and the next-neighbour products to $28/70$. The middle gate
retains the latter products. At the final gate mode $n=jk$ has
acquired $(-1)^j$, reversing the first harmonic. At $a=2a_*$ the
final gate returns the original $+4/5$. Every populated pair of
modes has a frequency difference at most $2ak^2/I$. As the sums of
absolute coefficient products are at most one, averaging over $s$
changes each moment from its nominal value by at most $1/50$ at
$a_*$ and $1/25$ at $2a_*$. The **raw mean cosine in the last gate**
therefore has opposite signs at the two scales, since $b_k>0$.
The dimensional calibrations are $m,d,\ell,T,\delta$, all fixed
independently of the trial action. The chosen resolution ratios
leave $m,d,T$ freely specifiable.

The same clock can also make two actions **exactly indistinguishable**.
Take $a_1=4\pi I/(k^2\delta)$ and $a_2=2a_1$. At either action,
every nonzero frequency difference between populated modes is an
integer multiple of $2\pi/\delta$. Averaging over a complete gate
removes all those terms in the position density. Coherences between
opposite modes of equal energy survive identically. Thus the success
rate and the entire blurred angle distribution in every gate coincide
for $a_1$ and $a_2$. The unrecorded time within a gate matters for
absolute identification.

Even allowing the preparation in a competing model to vary, the
three predictions at $a_*$ exclude zero under the stated dynamics.
After correcting for the known $b_q$, the distance

resolutions. The figures describe an attainable model preparation.

**Allowing radial motion.** Suppose the relative separation lies in a
calibrated annulus $d_-\le r\le d_+$ with $d_->0$. Let
$\beta=\sup_{[d_-,d_+]}|1/(\mu r^2)-1/I|$. For each classical
trajectory with $|L|\le L_{\max}$ the drift relative to rotation at
the constant rate $L/I$ is at most $t\beta L_{\max}$. Here $L$ is
conserved under any radial force. Thus the order-$q$ angle moment
changes by at most $t\beta |q|L_{\max}$. The additional dimensional
inputs are the two radial limits and a mechanical upper bound on
angular momentum, obtainable from an upper speed and radius.

For the canonical central Hamiltonian assume radial confinement in
that annulus, a common self-adjoint radial operator $H_r$ independent
of angle, and an initial state supported in $|L|\le L_{\max}$.
Compare $H_r+L^2/(2\mu r^2)$ with $H_r+L^2/(2I)$. The reference
angle mode at any time shifts $L$ by $aq$ and commutes with radial
multipliers. Its commutator with the Hamiltonian difference, divided
by $a$, has norm on the occupied sector at most
$\beta(|q|L_{\max}+a q^2/2)$. The Duhamel bound gives at most
$t\beta(|q|L_{\max}+a q^2/2)$ error per moment. Both evolutions
preserve the occupied sector, and the shifted sector also has a
bounded Hamiltonian difference. To exclude a whole trial interval
$0<a\le a_c$, use $a_c$ on the right and $t\le jT+\delta$ for each
gate. Enlarge the moment errors in (1) by these amounts. A mean bond
length alone supplies no such control. For an actual angle detector
the calibrated angular response must also hold throughout the annulus.

**What the ion experiment reads.** Glikin et al. prepared two rotating
calcium ions, applied three sideband pulses and read out mean
fluorescence. With sideband order $k$, total echo time $\tau$, and
angular momentum variance growth $2D$ per unit time, their ideal
signed fringe amplitude is

$$
 \cos\!\left(\frac{a k^2\tau}{2I}\right)
 \exp\!\left[-\frac{D\tau}{2a^2}
       \left(1-\operatorname{sinc}\frac{ak\tau}{I}\right)\right].
                                                                    \tag{3}
$$

The paper includes the cosine when fitting its weak-noise,
third-order measurements. It gives 2.1 ms for the first ideal node
and 2.4 ms after simulating pulse imperfections. These are predicted
node times. The measured quantity is the contrast of an electronic
phase scan. [Glikin et al., Fig. 3, Eq. (A53) and Appendix B, Fig. 5](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.13293#page=11)
(passages).

A classical rotor can reproduce the entire ideal mean fringe. Give
the apparatus a mechanical angular impulse scale $J>0$ and the rotor
arbitrary initial angle and speed. Between pulses apply independent
random impulses $\pm 2J$ with equal sign probabilities at total
Poisson rate $D/(2J^2)$. The angular momentum variance growth is
$2D$. At the middle pulse add an independent impulse $\pm kJ$ with
equal probabilities. Let a classical phase recorder accumulate
$k[\theta(0)-2\theta(\tau/2)+\theta(\tau)]$ and give a bright
probability $(1-\cos(\phi+\text{accumulated phase}))/2$ for analyzer
phase $\phi$. It samples the same initial ensemble for all $\phi$.
An impulse at $u$ contributes its impulse times
$k\min(u,\tau-u)/I$ to the accumulated phase. The middle pulse
therefore contributes the cosine of (3) with $a=J$. The
characteristic function of the independent Poisson impulses gives
the exponential there, since the time average of
$\cos[2Jk\min(u,\tau-u)/I]$ is $\operatorname{sinc}(Jk\tau/I)$.
[Last and Penrose, Theorem 3.9 and Proposition 5.4](https://stoch.math.kit.edu/img/Last/lastpenrose2017.pdf)
(passages, Poisson functional and independent marking).

This alternative carries zero wave action and a finite **apparatus
impulse** $J$. Its independent dimensional specifications are $I$,
$J$, $D$ and $\tau$. It makes a further prediction at fixed diffusion
strength. With the middle pulse absent, the fourth cumulant of the
angular momentum increment in time $t$ is $8DJ^2t$. Gaussian torque
increments have zero fourth cumulant. Matching the mean fluorescence
and the diffusion rate leaves this independent force measurement
open. The direct angle test uses a single terminal exposure and
removes the middle-pulse impulse altogether.

**The trajectory test.** For any two real phases $X,Y$ the real
part of the three-point characteristic function obeys

$$
 \cos X+\cos Y-\cos(X+Y)\le 3/2 .                              \tag{4}
$$

This is the three-point Bochner positivity test, obtained by taking
the squared modulus of $1-e^{iX}-e^{-iY}$. Averaging retains the
bound for every joint distribution. Even a common affine electronic
readout $c\mapsto g c+c_0$ preserves the ceiling when $g\ge0$ and
$g+|c_0|\le1$, since $W\mapsto gW+c_0$.
Finite characteristic-function tests of Wigner positivity, including
tests with conditional displacements and a qubit readout, are established.
Here the question is whether the ion's probe reads the same mechanical
phases that these inequalities constrain.
[Narcowich and O'Connell](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.34.1),
[Mari et al.](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.106.010403)
and [Zaw](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.133.050201) (abstracts).

A force law gives (4) a direct mechanical interpretation. Choose one
Cartesian coordinate of a central Hooke orbit with inertial mass $m$
and angular frequency $\omega$. Set the origin at the force centre.
Expose one independently chosen terminal gate per release at
$t_0,t_0+\pi/(4\omega),t_0+\pi/(2\omega)$. Let the positions be
$x_-,x_m,x_+$. For every harmonic trajectory
$x_-+x_+=\sqrt{2}\,x_m$. Measure cosines of $kx_-,kx_+$ and
$\sqrt{2}kx_m$ respectively, with $k=2\pi/\Lambda$ set by a ruler
pitch $\Lambda$. Choosing the phase origin fixes which two
quarter-period projections are the authors' axes. The construction
requires the same release law at all gates. It allows arbitrary
initial position–velocity correlations. If the three gates have the
same distribution of clock offsets independent of the release, their
marginals can be coupled using one common offset. A centred Gaussian
position error of the same width $\ell$ at each gate preserves (4)
for the **raw** cosines.
Couple independent endpoint errors $\xi,\zeta$ of width $\ell$ to the
middle error $(\xi+\zeta)/\sqrt{2}$, of the same width.

Newton's force measurement sets a tolerance here. Suppose independent
displacement measurements bound $|\ddot x+\omega^2 x|\le b_F$ on the
admitted trajectories. About the middle gate the sine response of the
oscillator integrated on each side gives

$$
 |x_-+x_+-\sqrt{2}x_m|\le (2-\sqrt{2})b_F/\omega^2 .
$$

Thus the maximum excess attributable to the force error is
$|k|(2-\sqrt{2})b_F/\omega^2$. To compare canonical models on the
same footing, let $a>0$ be the trial reduced action and take a common
midpoint state, a self-adjoint harmonic-plus-potential evolution and
a bounded perturbation of slope
at most $mb_F$. The propagated harmonic endpoint mode translates the
perturbation's argument by a distance $|ak\sin(\omega u)|/(m\omega)$
at lag $u$. The commutator divided by $a$ is bounded by
$|k|b_F|\sin(\omega u)|/\omega$. The two endpoint Duhamel integrals
give the same combined tolerance. The Gaussian error only contracts
each Fourier mode. Remaining timing, selection and readout errors need
their own bounds. With direct Gaussian-blurred positions a verified
excess beyond the errors would require $a>2m\omega\ell^2$ in this
family. Smoothing the midpoint Wigner distribution with widths
$\ell,m\omega\ell$ gives a positive joint law for every state when
$a\le2m\omega\ell^2$, by the Gaussian smoothing result used
[above](#a-three-time-test-with-a-calibrated-position-resolution).
All these statements survive refinement between the physical gates.

**The action in a calibrated phase measurement.** For ideal, unit-gain
linear probes put $X=kQ$, $Y=kP/(m\omega)$ in coordinates referred to
the first gate. In a canonical model the Weyl phase is
$\Phi=ak^2/(2m\omega)$. Positivity of the squared norms of
$1-e^{iX}-e^{-iY}$ and $1-e^{-iX}-e^{iY}$, averaged over the two
signs and the state, gives

$$
                  A+B-C\cos\Phi\le 3/2 .                         \tag{5}
$$

Here $C=\operatorname{Re}\langle e^{i(X+Y)}\rangle$. The ordered
products carry opposite Weyl phases in the two real-part bounds.
Averaging removes the unmeasured imaginary component. This is a
finite instance of quantum characteristic-function positivity. At the
$0.37$ point estimates (5) requires
$\cos\Phi\le(1.5-1.312)/0.456=47/114$ and hence

$$
       a\ \ge\ \frac{2m\omega}{k^2}\arccos(47/114) .              \tag{6}
$$

For uncertain data use the weakest bound over their joint error set.
The dimensional inputs for (6) are the mechanical mass, period and
ruler pitch. Direct exposures additionally use the clock response,
point-spread width and any residual acceleration bound. The experiment
gives the trap clock, uses 355 nm Raman light, and quotes a calibrated
sideband rate $\Omega/(2\pi)=2$ kHz and a $141\ \mu$s axis pulse.
The optical wavelength and the effective motional fringe pitch are
different calibrations. In the oscillator convention
$k=b/x_{\rm zp}$, $x_{\rm zp}=\sqrt{a/(2m\omega)}$, which makes
$\Phi=b^2$ at every positive trial action. Holding the dimensionless
pulse settings and preparation fixed therefore leaves their ideal
records invariant under $a\mapsto\lambda a$ with spatial widths
multiplied by $\sqrt\lambda$. A translation by a mechanically known
distance, with the analyzer phase scanned, can calibrate $k$ directly.
Using $x_{\rm zp}$ as that ruler would insert the action being tested.
[Valahu et al., Methods and supplement II C](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04865)
(passages on the sideband drive and pulse duration).

**A zero-action mechanical alternative.** The following single probe
law reproduces all four entries in each row, including the measured
origin. Use the harmonic orbit with classical quadratures $X=kQ$ and
$Y=kP/(m\omega)$. Before reading the setting $(u,v)$ in units of $b$,
give it a random momentum impulse $\pm uJ$ with equiprobable signs
and $J=\pi m\omega/k$ ($k>0$). The meter reads the cosine of
$uX+vY\pm\pi uv$. Both axis phases are intact and the diagonal
cosine changes sign. The impulse has zero mean. Translating the orbit
changes each probe phase by precisely the amount prescribed by its
linear slope, including on the diagonal.

For each preparation independently choose a tag sign of mean $V$
which multiplies the output. Choose signs $s,t=\pm1$ with probabilities

$$
           p_{st}=\frac{V+sA+tB-stC}{4V}.
$$

Set $X,Y$ to $0$ or $\pi$ so that their cosines are $s,t$. These are
four ordinary initial points of the harmonic flow. The probabilities
sum to one and are nonnegative for every row. The smallest numerator
in the $0.30$ row is $0.928-0.616-0.676+0.380=0.016$.
Consequently the mean origin, axis and diagonal outputs are precisely
$V,A,B,C$. The model uses the same impulse and readout rule at all
three preparations. Its scope is the four selected settings per row.

**What repeated probes can resolve.** At ideal lattice spacing the
axis Weyl operators $U=e^{iX}$ and $V_p=e^{iY}$ commute, with
$[X,Y]=2\pi i$. Their joint spectral probabilities define a positive
law of phases $(x,y)$ for every incident quantum state. At integer
$(u,v)$ the Weyl multiplication rule gives the signal
$(-1)^{uv}\mathbb E e^{i(ux+vy)}$, including the diagonal sign.
The classical kick just constructed gives this sign for every integer
pair. Complete the other axis probe by shifting $Q$ by $\pi/k$ after
it is read. Both probes then move the conjugate phase through half a
fringe, and give the full ideal sequence of axial records.
In fact the symmetric axial measurement has a factor
$(1+r e^{i\theta}U)/2$ for a bit $r=\pm1$ and a half displacement.
The first factor weights the joint law by
$(1+r\cos(x+\theta))/2$. Conjugation by the half displacement changes
$V_p$ to $-V_p$. Thus the classical rule samples the bit and sends
$y$ to $y+\pi$ modulo $2\pi$. Exchanging the two axes gives the
other rule. Iteration covers each bit string, including analyzer phases
chosen from earlier bits. The comparison concerns the ideal axial
instruments and the exact lattice. Valahu et al. explicitly include
this bit flip in the analysis of their sequential sensing protocol.
[Supplement, Eqs. (S.52), (S.69) and (S.71)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.04865#page=26)
(passages).

## A force transmitted between two ions

In a modern test of the force measurement, give ion 2 a small transverse
impulse $J$ and measure ion 1's displacement at time $t$. For two equal
inertial masses $m$ in a harmonic trap the impulse response is

$$
\chi_{12}(t)=\frac{\delta x_1(t)}{J}
=\frac{1}{2m}\left(\frac{\sin\omega_C t}{\omega_C}
                    -\frac{\sin\omega_R t}{\omega_R}\right).
$$

Here $C,R$ denote the in-phase and relative modes. The displacements
of the two modes initially cancel at ion 1. The first surviving term
is $-(\omega_C^2-\omega_R^2)t^3/(12m)$. The force gradient between the
ions is measurable through this delayed response. Newton's Lemma X
concerns displacement of the body on which the force acts directly.
This extension specifies where a second body can register the force.
The impulse can be calibrated as $m$ times an observed change in
velocity. Alternatively, the two periods fix $m\chi_{12}$.
[Reciprocal mechanical calibration](rotation-composition-universality.md#reciprocal-mechanical-calibration)
(passage, mass normalization).

**An observed spin readout.** Wong-Campos et al. prepared both ion
spins by optical pumping and a microwave rotation, applied ten optical
kicks, and used microwave analysis and separate fluorescence detectors.
They reported Bell fidelity $F_{\rm obs}=0.76(1)$ and parity contrast
$0.69(1)$. The fidelity is half the sum of the even-spin population and
the contrast. Separable spins have Bell fidelity at most $1/2$ for
trusted local spin analysis. The reported uncertainties are statistical.
[Wong-Campos et al., Figs. 1–2 and Eqs. (1)–(7)](https://link.aps.org/accepted/10.1103/PhysRevLett.119.230501)
and [White et al., Appendix B](https://quantum.prof/publications/2007/measuringGates.pdf)
(passages).

**What each optical kick fixes.** Let $\kappa$ be the difference of
the two beam wave vectors projected on the motional axis. It is
$4\pi/\lambda$ for collinear counterpropagating beams of equal
wavelength. A coherent spin flip at position $x$ carries the measured
optical phase $\pm\kappa x$ up to a position-independent phase. This
position-local pulse law is a physical assumption that can be tested
by scanning spin phase against a ruler. The corresponding mechanical
impulse is an output of the model. The calculation uses neither a photon
momentum nor an oscillator ground-state width as a calibration.
[Clocked grating, preparation and readout](groupoid-quantum-newton-states.md#preparation-and-readout)
(passage).

Prepare separable spins independently of the joint motional state.
The two modes act on distinct factors and may be correlated in that
state. In the interaction picture require the calibrated harmonic
response for positions as observables,

$$
Q_\mu(t)=Q_\mu\cos(\omega_\mu t)
        +\frac{P_\mu}{m\omega_\mu}\sin(\omega_\mu t),
\qquad
x_{1,2}(t)=\frac{Q_C(t)\pm Q_R(t)}{\sqrt2}.
$$

A centroid check on a single state is weaker than this dynamical
premise. Remove the known local spin flips and spatially constant phases.
For a spin branch $z_1,z_2=\pm1$, kick $n$ at $t_n$ then acts by
$e^{iB_n}$ with $B_n=s_n\kappa[z_1x_1(t_n)+z_2x_2(t_n)]$.
The signs $s_n=\pm1$ include the effect of all preceding spin flips.
Allow a commutative motional algebra as well as regular operator
realizations. Write $K_\mu=\|[Q_\mu,P_\mu]\|$. For the norm estimate
take the commutators bounded, and the real linear combinations and
partial sums self-adjoint on closure from a common invariant core on
which their unitary groups permit Duhamel differentiation. No scalar
commutator is required.

Replace the ordered product on each branch by $e^{i\sum_n B_n}$.
With $L_\mu=\kappa\sum_n s_n Q_\mu(t_n)/\sqrt2$, the replacement applies
local spin rotations with angles given by the two commuting observables
$L_C+L_R$ and $L_C-L_R$. Tracing motion averages product rotations
over their joint spectral measure. Thus the replacement leaves the
spins separable for any admitted motional preparation, including a
trajectory that fails to return to its initial point.

On each branch the replacement has norm error at most
$\tfrac12\sum_{j<n}\|[B_n,B_j]\|$. This uses the unitary Lie product
bound for each partial sum.
[Iserles and Kropielnicka, Theorem 2.1 and §3.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.06635#page=5)
(passage). Equal spin signs select $C$, opposite signs select $R$.
In the selected mode $B_n=\pm\sqrt2\,s_n\kappa Q_\mu(t_n)$, and
$[Q_\mu(t_j),Q_\mu(t_n)]=[Q_\mu,P_\mu]\sin(\omega_\mu d_{nj})/(m\omega_\mu)$
with $d_{nj}=t_n-t_j>0$. The norm error bounds the change in a Bell
probability even after tracing the motion. Define

$$
D_\mu=\sum_{j<n}\frac{|\sin(\omega_\mu d_{nj})|}{\omega_\mu},
\qquad
B_{\rm ion}=\frac{\kappa^2}{m}\max_{\mu=C,R} K_\mu D_\mu .
$$

Let $F_{\rm low}$ be a statistical lower limit, and $e$ an independently
justified upper bound on the upward shift of the Bell probability from
preparation, readout and dynamics outside this model. Put
$\Delta=F_{\rm low}-1/2-e$. For a prescribed optical reference,
$\Delta\le B_{\rm ion}$. In particular, for $N\ge2$ kicks,

$$
\boxed{\quad \Delta>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{\max_\mu K_\mu}{m}\ \ge\
\frac{\omega_{\min}\Delta}{\kappa^2\binom{N}{2}}>0,\qquad
\omega_{\min}=\min(\omega_C,\omega_R).\quad}
$$

This bounds the norm of a mechanical commutator in units of action,
uniformly over initial motional states. For regular canonical modes
with common trial value $[Q_\mu,P_\mu]=iaI$ it bounds $|a|$.
The signed phase difference between equal- and opposite-spin branches
at motional reunion is then
$\Phi=2a\kappa^2\sum_{j<n}s_js_n\chi_{12}(d_{nj})$, by the cited
gate formula, Eq. (3). Write $\Lambda=\Phi/a$. If $\Lambda\ne0$, the
two positive values $a$ and $a+2\pi/|\Lambda|$ give identical spin
statistics for this one closed schedule and the same mechanical impulse

## Matched endpoints, arbitrary partitions and action normalization

For $L=m|\dot q|^2/2+Fy$, the same-endpoint chord has
$y_C(t)=F\tau t/(2m)$. If $y=y_F+\eta$ and both endpoint variations
vanish, integration by parts using $m\ddot y_F=F$ gives

$$S[y_F+\eta]-S[y_F]=\frac m2\int_0^\tau\dot\eta^2dt.$$

For $\eta=Ft(\tau-t)/(2m)$ this is

$$D_{C,F}=\frac{F^2\tau^3}{24m}
 =\frac{F}{2v}A_{\rm chord,fall}
 =\frac{F}{4v}A_{\rm inertial,fall}=\frac{\tau\Delta E}{12}.$$

A common ordinary point-function term $dG(q,t)/dt$ added to $L$ cancels
because the endpoints and times agree. A velocity-dependent $G$ changes
the endpoint data required by that statement: adding
$d(\kappa\dot y)/dt$ changes this chord-minus-parabola action by
$-\kappa F\tau/m$, since their endpoint velocities differ. That is a
higher-derivative Lagrangian, outside this point-mechanics convention.

endpoints; an observable phase comparison needs endpoint/control phases.

## A genuine receding centre: subtract before taking the limit

$$A_R[\gamma]=\frac12\int_\gamma[x\,dy-(y-R)\,dx]

## Controlled reunion of the inertial and falling arms

Keep a reference arm at zero transverse force and apply to the signal arm

$$f(t)=\begin{cases}F,&0<t<\tau,\\-F,&\tau<t<3\tau,\\
F,&3\tau<t<4\tau.\end{cases}$$

Both begin at $y=\dot y=0$. This is branch-controlled motion; gravity needs
compensation on the reference and return stages. Define
$P(t)=\int_0^tf$, $Y(t)=m^{-1}\int_0^tP$. Then $P(4\tau)=Y(4\tau)=0$;
$P/F$ is respectively $t$, $2\tau-t$ and $t-4\tau$. Direct integration gives

$$\int_0^{4\tau}P^2dt=\tfrac43F^2\tau^3,\qquad
\int_0^{4\tau}Ydt=2F\tau^3/m,\qquad
A_{\rm loop}=2vF\tau^3/m=12A_{\rm inertial,fall}.$$

$Y\ge0$ peaks at $F\tau^2/m$ at $2\tau$. Using $m\ddot Y=f$ and the
vanishing boundary term $mY\dot Y$,

$$\Delta S_{\rm loop}=-\frac1{2m}\int P^2dt
 =-\frac{2F^2\tau^3}{3m}=-\frac F{3v}A_{\rm loop}
 =-\frac43\tau\Delta E.$$

The sign and coefficient differ from the off-shell chord defect because
return forces are part of this experiment. Its exact comparison with the

$$[B(t),B(s)]=\frac{if(t)f(s)(t-s)}{m\hbar}.$$

 =\exp\left[-\frac{i}{2m\hbar}\int_0^{4\tau}P^2dt\right]I
